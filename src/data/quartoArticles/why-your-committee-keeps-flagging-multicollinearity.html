The Comment That Shows Up in Almost Every Regression Chapter Review

If you’ve submitted a regression analysis to a dissertation committee and gotten it back with a comment about “multicollinearity” or “variance inflation,” you’re in extremely common company. It’s one of the most frequently raised issues in quantitative dissertation reviews across social science, education, business, and health fields — and it’s also one of the most misunderstood, because the term sounds far more intimidating than the underlying problem actually is.

Here’s the plain-language version: multicollinearity happens when two or more of the variables you’re using to predict an outcome are themselves closely related to each other. Your regression model can’t tell which of the overlapping variables deserves the credit for the relationship it’s finding, so it starts producing unstable, sometimes contradictory results — a variable that should logically have a positive relationship with your outcome shows up with a negative coefficient, or a variable you’re confident matters shows no statistical significance at all, not because it doesn’t matter, but because a closely related variable in the same model is absorbing its explanatory power.

Why This Shows Up So Often in Dissertation Data Specifically

Multicollinearity isn’t a sign of sloppy research. It’s an almost inevitable consequence of how survey and organizational data actually gets collected. Researcher Bruce Thompson’s widely cited analysis of methodological errors across dissertations lists insufficient attention to multivariate relationships as one of seven recurring principles dissertation researchers get wrong — and multicollinearity is the specific, mechanical way that problem shows up when a candidate builds a regression model without checking how their predictor variables relate to each other first.

Consider a common dissertation scenario: a study examining what predicts employee turnover intention, using job satisfaction, organizational commitment, and work engagement as three separate predictors. These three constructs are measured with different survey instruments and treated as conceptually distinct in the literature review. But in practice, they tend to be strongly correlated with each other in real survey data — an employee who reports high job satisfaction also tends to report high organizational commitment and high engagement, because all three are, to some degree, capturing overlapping aspects of how someone feels about their job. Put all three into one regression model and the model can no longer cleanly separate their individual effects, even though each one, on its own, might show a clear relationship with turnover intention.

How to Actually Check for It (Before Your Committee Does)

The standard diagnostic is called the Variance Inflation Factor, or VIF — a single number, calculated for each predictor variable in your model, that tells you how much that variable’s relationship with your outcome is being distorted by its correlation with your other predictors. A VIF close to 1 means little to no problem. Values above 5, and especially above 10, are widely treated as a warning sign that a model has a real multicollinearity issue that needs to be addressed before the results can be trusted.

Most standard statistical software — SPSS, R, Stata, Python’s statsmodels — can calculate VIF with a single additional command alongside a standard regression. There is no reasonable excuse for a Chapter 4 to reach a committee without this check having been run and reported, because the check itself takes minutes and the absence of it is one of the first things a quantitatively trained committee member will look for.

What to Actually Do When You Find It

Finding a multicollinearity problem doesn’t mean your model, or your study, is broken. It means you have a specific, well-documented decision to make, and several defensible options for making it:

  • Combine the overlapping variables into a single composite measure, if your theoretical framework supports treating them as one underlying construct rather than several distinct ones. This is often the cleanest fix when, as in the turnover example above, the overlapping variables are conceptually close to begin with.
  • Drop one or more of the redundant variables, keeping the one most directly tied to your specific research question, and explicitly justify that choice in your methodology — reviewers respond far better to an acknowledged, reasoned trade-off than to an unaddressed VIF flag.
  • Use a regularized regression technique — Ridge, Lasso, or Elastic Net — which is specifically designed to handle correlated predictors by penalizing the model for leaning too hard on any single overlapping variable, rather than requiring you to remove variables by hand. This is a more advanced fix, but it’s increasingly accepted in quantitative dissertations precisely because it’s a well-documented, defensible solution to exactly this problem rather than an ad hoc workaround.
  • Report the multicollinearity check explicitly in your results section, even if your VIF values are fine. A committee member who doesn’t see the check reported has no way to know whether it wasn’t a problem or simply wasn’t checked — and the second interpretation is the one that generates a revision request.

Why Catching This Early Matters More Than It Seems

A regression model with an unaddressed multicollinearity problem doesn’t just risk a critical committee comment. It risks something more specific and more damaging: a finding that looks statistically clean in a first draft but falls apart, or reverses direction, when a committee member asks you to rerun the model with one variable removed — which is exactly the kind of question a multicollinearity-aware reviewer asks first. Catching and addressing it before the defense, rather than being asked to address it live in front of your committee, is the difference between a planned revision and an uncomfortable, unplanned one.
