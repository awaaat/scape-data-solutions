The Book That Named the Problem

In 1978, mathematician and educator Sheila Tobias published Overcoming Math Anxiety, a book built on a simple, radical observation. She had spent years watching capable, intelligent adults, many of them successful in every other part of their academic or professional life, freeze up, avoid, or quietly opt out the moment a task involved numbers. The prevailing explanation at the time was some version of aptitude, that these people simply weren’t “math people.” Tobias’s research pointed somewhere else entirely: the block wasn’t ability, it was anxiety, often traceable to a specific early experience, a harsh teacher, a public failure, a moment of being timed and humiliated, that had nothing to do with actual mathematical capability and everything to do with what happened in the body the next time numbers showed up.

Tobias’s book gave the problem a name and, more importantly, made it something other than a personal failing. But it took almost two more decades of research to explain the actual mechanism. In the 1990s and 2000s, cognitive psychologist Mark Ashcraft ran a series of studies that found something precise: math anxiety measurably reduces the capacity of working memory, the exact mental resource a person needs to hold numbers in mind while manipulating them. In his studies, anxious individuals performed nearly identically to non anxious individuals on simple, automatic calculations, but fell dramatically behind on tasks requiring them to hold information in mind while working through several steps, precisely the kind of task a statistics chapter demands constantly. The anxiety wasn’t a symptom of struggling with the material. It was actively using up the mental capacity needed to do the work in the first place.

That distinction matters enormously for anyone staring at a stalled chapter 4. The feeling of statistics being disproportionately hard isn’t evidence of a missing aptitude. For a huge share of people who experience it, it’s a measurable, physiological tax on working memory, triggered by anxiety, that shows up specifically when the task requires holding several things in mind at once, which describes almost every step of a regression or factor analysis chapter.

It’s a Working Memory Problem, Not an Ability Problem

Ashcraft’s research is worth sitting with for a specific reason: it means the frustration of rereading the same paragraph about interaction effects five times isn’t a sign the material is beyond you. It’s what reduced working memory capacity looks like in practice, and it happens to plenty of people who are perfectly capable of the underlying reasoning once the anxiety isn’t actively competing for the same mental space.

This has a direct, practical implication. Anything that reduces the anxiety in the moment, before attempting the hardest part of the work, frees up working memory that was otherwise being spent managing the anxiety response rather than the statistics. This is why working through a worked example step by step, in a low stakes way, before attempting the real analysis, consistently helps more than simply trying harder on the real thing. The worked example lowers the stakes enough that anxiety doesn’t consume the working memory needed to actually follow the logic.

The Avoidance Spiral

Anxiety around a task doesn’t just make the task harder in the moment. It creates a behavioral pattern that makes the next attempt harder too. A person who feels overwhelmed by chapter 4 tends to avoid opening the document, which means the next time they do open it, they’ve lost the thread of where they were, which makes the material feel even more overwhelming, which increases the anxiety, which increases the avoidance. Tobias documented this spiral directly in the adults she studied, many of whom had avoided anything numerical for years by the time they sought her out, not because the material had gotten harder, but because each avoided attempt made the next one feel more daunting than the last.

Breaking that spiral rarely works by pushing harder against it. It tends to work by shrinking the task small enough that starting doesn’t trigger the same anxiety response, a single variable’s descriptive statistics instead of the full analysis, a single paragraph instead of the full section, done consistently enough that the avoidance pattern doesn’t have room to take hold.

What Actually Helps

The research building on Tobias and Ashcraft’s work points toward a small set of concrete practices, not motivational advice. Working through fully worked examples before attempting original analysis reduces the cognitive load of the real task, because the anxiety producing uncertainty of not knowing what a correct answer even looks like has already been resolved once. Breaking a chapter into the smallest possible units, one test, one table, one paragraph, rather than treating the whole chapter as one task, keeps each individual attempt small enough that the anxiety response has less to attach to. And working with another person present, whether a statistics consultant, a writing group, or a peer, consistently shows up in the research as reducing the anxiety response on its own, independent of whether that person is even actively helping with the math, simply because isolation tends to amplify the spiral Tobias described.

  • Treat the anxiety as a working memory tax, not a sign you can’t do this. Ashcraft’s research found capable people perform normally on simple steps and fall behind specifically on multi step tasks under anxiety, which describes almost every statistics chapter.
  • Work a fully worked example before attempting your real analysis. Removing the uncertainty of what a correct answer looks like frees up the working memory the real task actually needs.
  • Shrink the task until starting doesn’t trigger the spiral. One test, one table, one paragraph, done consistently, breaks the avoidance pattern Tobias documented far more reliably than trying to push through the whole chapter at once.
