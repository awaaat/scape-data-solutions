The Growth Number That Hid the Real One

In 2012, a home cleaning startup called Homejoy launched with a simple pitch: book a professional house cleaning through an app, pay a flat rate, done. The company grew fast. A first time cleaning cost as little as nineteen dollars, heavily subsidized, and that price made new customer acquisition almost frictionless. Bookings climbed, press coverage followed, and Homejoy raised roughly forty million dollars from investors including Google Ventures, largely on the strength of that growth curve.

The number that mattered far more than bookings never made it into most of the early coverage. Later reporting on the company’s internal data found that only somewhere around 15 to 20 percent of customers who tried Homejoy ever booked a second cleaning at full price. The nineteen dollar first clean wasn’t bringing in customers who stuck around, it was bringing in customers who wanted a discounted cleaning once. The headline growth number and the actual retention number were describing two almost unrelated businesses, and for a long stretch, only one of them was on the dashboard anyone was watching closely.

By 2015, the math caught up. Homejoy had spent heavily to keep the acquisition number climbing, the repeat business never materialized at the scale the model needed, and the company was also facing lawsuits over how it classified its cleaners as independent contractors. Unable to raise another round on numbers that no longer told a convincing growth story once retention was factored in, Homejoy shut down in July 2015. It remains one of the standard cautionary examples used in startup circles for a specific failure: a single top line metric that looked healthy while the number underneath it, the one that actually determined whether the business worked, was quietly telling a different story.

Homejoy didn’t fail because customers didn’t like the service. It failed for three specific, unglamorous reasons that show up in a much smaller form on a SaaS founder’s churn dashboard every month. A blended headline number hid which customers were actually staying and which were leaving. An asset the company already had, its existing customer base and the data inside it, went largely untracked while attention stayed on acquisition. And when it mattered most, the company kept spending to grow the easy number instead of fixing the harder problem that determined whether any of that growth would last.

The Single Number That Hides Who’s Actually Leaving

B2B SaaS companies report average annual churn somewhere between 3.5% and 4.9%, depending on the benchmark and segment. That single figure is the number most boards ask about, and it’s also one of the least actionable numbers in the business, because it treats every lost account as interchangeable. It’s the same structural blind spot that let Homejoy’s bookings number look healthy while the repeat rate underneath it told a completely different story.

A large and growing body of peer reviewed research backs up why that blended number is so misleading. A 2025 systematic review published in Machine Learning and Knowledge Extraction, screening more than 800 studies down to 61 for detailed analysis, confirmed what churn researchers have converged on across industries: churn is highly predictable from behavioral signals well before a customer actually cancels, declining login frequency, reduced feature usage, and support ticket patterns consistently show up as leading indicators across the literature. A separate 2023 study in PLOS ONE built a churn prediction model on real banking customer data and went a step further than just predicting who would leave, it used model interpretability techniques to explain why the model flagged each customer, turning a black box prediction into a specific, actionable reason. That distinction matters enormously for a SaaS business: knowing a customer has an 80 percent chance of churning is only useful if you also know it’s because their usage of a specific core feature dropped 60 percent in the last billing cycle, not some generic risk score.

Homejoy had exactly this kind of signal sitting in its own booking data from close to day one. A customer who didn’t rebook within a few weeks of their discounted first clean was, in hindsight, an obvious churn signal. The company had the data. What it didn’t have was a dashboard built to surface that signal instead of the acquisition number that looked better in a pitch deck.

The Customer Base Sitting Untracked

Homejoy’s real asset was never its acquisition funnel. It was the pool of customers who had already tried the service, people who had already cleared the hardest part of any customer relationship, the first purchase, and simply needed a reason to come back. The company poured resources into finding new versions of that same person over and over through discounted first cleans, rather than treating the customers it already had as the asset actually worth building the business around.

SaaS companies carry a version of the same untracked asset inside net revenue retention, and it’s often hidden by construction rather than by neglect. Net revenue retention, or NRR, combines two very different things into one figure: revenue lost to churned and downgraded accounts, and revenue gained from expansion and upsells among customers who stayed. A company can post a comfortable 105 percent NRR while quietly losing a meaningful share of its logo base, as long as expansion revenue from the accounts that stayed is large enough to mask it. Rising churn alongside flat revenue churn typically means a company is shedding small accounts while retaining large ones, a pattern that looks fine on the NRR line and terrible on the logo count line, and the two metrics can tell completely different stories in the same quarter, the same way Homejoy’s bookings and repeat rate told different stories.

This is the SaaS version of the trap that shows up across every business with more than one signal moving at once. Product usage, NPS score, support ticket volume, and expansion revenue all tend to rise and fall together for a healthy account and fall together for an unhealthy one, which makes it tempting to treat them as one combined health score without checking which individual signal is actually doing the predictive work. The equivalent mistake in SaaS is assuming a rising feature adoption number is causing retention, when both might simply be downstream of a genuinely engaged customer segment that would have stayed regardless, the same way Homejoy’s early growth numbers looked like proof of product market fit when they were really just proof that a steep discount moves bookings.

The practical fix mirrors what customer experience researchers building account level churn tools have converged on: predict and act on churn risk at the individual account level using specific behavioral signals, rather than reading retention health off one blended, top line number.

Spending to Grow the Easy Number Instead of Fixing the Hard One

Homejoy’s leadership wasn’t unaware that repeat business was weak. Multiple accounts from people close to the company describe a strategy of continuing to spend on new customer acquisition to keep growth numbers climbing while the harder, less glamorous work of improving retention lagged behind. Acquiring a new customer at a steep discount was a lever the company could pull immediately and see results from within days. Fixing whatever was causing three quarters of first time customers to never come back was slower, harder to measure, and didn’t show up as a satisfying line going up in a board deck the following week. The company kept choosing the lever that was easy to pull over the one that actually determined whether the business worked.

Every SaaS founder eventually has to answer a board question that sounds simple and isn’t: what will ARR be next quarter? The honest answer depends on at least three uncertain numbers at once, new bookings, churn rate, and expansion revenue, each of which moves independently and none of which is known in advance. Douglas Hubbard’s How to Measure Anything: Finding the Value of Intangibles in Business makes the case that presenting a single point forecast for a number driven by multiple uncertain inputs is one of the more common ways businesses mislead themselves and their boards, not through dishonesty, but through false precision. The fix, Monte Carlo simulation, is available in an ordinary spreadsheet: instead of guessing one ARR figure, a founder describes a realistic range for churn, a range for new bookings, and a range for expansion revenue, then runs those ranges through thousands of randomized combinations. What comes back is a full distribution, a most likely ARR figure, but also the odds of landing meaningfully below plan, which is usually the number that actually matters for a hiring or spending decision made this quarter. A board looking only at a single optimistic bookings projection is looking at the same kind of number Homejoy’s early investors were looking at, technically accurate and functionally misleading.

What Homejoy’s Collapse Actually Teaches

Homejoy did not fail because customers disliked the service or because a competitor out executed it. It failed because a company with real usage data sitting in its own systems kept watching the number that looked good instead of the number that mattered, let its actual asset, an already acquired customer base, go untracked, and kept spending to grow the easy metric instead of fixing the hard problem underneath it. No single decision sank the company. All three, compounding over about three years, did.

None of that is a SaaS specific problem. It’s what happens to any subscription or repeat purchase business that has a headline metric simple enough to put in a pitch deck and a real retention story that’s harder to see and harder to fix. The version that shows up on a SaaS churn dashboard is smaller and slower moving than Homejoy’s collapse, but it runs on exactly the same mechanism, and it stays invisible for exactly the same reason, because the easy number keeps looking fine right up until it doesn’t.

  • Split your churn number by account size and by cohort before you present it. A flat blended churn rate can hide a small account bleed sitting underneath a healthy looking NRR, in the same way Homejoy’s blended bookings number hid a repeat rate under 20 percent. Check whether logo churn and revenue churn are telling the same story or diverging.
  • Rank your usage signals instead of averaging them into a single health score. The PLOS ONE and Machine Learning and Knowledge Extraction research both point the same direction: a handful of specific behavioral signals, login frequency, core feature usage, support patterns, do most of the predictive work, and a blended score can bury the one that actually matters for a given account.
  • Track repeat and expansion behavior inside your existing customer base as its own metric, not a footnote to acquisition. Homejoy’s real business was hiding inside its repeat customer data the entire time. A SaaS company’s equivalent, expansion and reactivation inside accounts already won, is usually cheaper to grow than new logo acquisition and gets a fraction of the attention.
  • Before presenting a single ARR number to your board, run it as a range. Even a rough, three scenario version, a bad case, a likely case, and a good case, gives your board a more honest and more useful answer than a single confident figure, and it surfaces which input, churn, bookings, or expansion, actually deserves the most attention this quarter.

If any of this sounds like a dashboard problem rather than a metrics problem, that’s usually because it is. Homejoy didn’t need a better growth number to survive. It needed to look honestly at the retention number sitting right next to it, on a timeline that mattered.
