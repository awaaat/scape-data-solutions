The Churn Number That Explains Nothing

A SaaS founder pulls up the board deck the night before a quarterly meeting. Churn is 4.1% this month, up from 3.6% last month. Net revenue retention is 98%, down two points. Both numbers are correct. Neither one tells him which customers are leaving, why, or what to do differently before next month’s number comes in the same way.

This is the recurring gap in subscription businesses, and it isn’t a tooling problem. It’s the same gap that shows up in every business built on repeat relationships, distilled into two fears:

  1. Am I losing customers I should be keeping?
  2. Am I missing revenue I should be capturing — specifically, expansion revenue sitting inside the accounts I already have?

Fear #1: A Single Churn Number Hides Which Customers Are Actually at Risk

B2B SaaS companies report average annual churn somewhere between 3.5% and 4.9%, depending on the benchmark and segment. That single figure is the number most boards ask about — and it’s also the least actionable number in the business, because it treats every lost account as interchangeable.

A large and growing body of peer-reviewed research disagrees with that framing. A 2025 systematic review published in Machine Learning and Knowledge Extraction, screening more than 800 studies down to 61 for detailed analysis, confirmed what churn researchers have converged on across industries: churn is highly predictable from behavioral signals well before a customer actually cancels — declining login frequency, reduced feature usage, and support-ticket patterns consistently show up as leading indicators across the literature. A separate 2023 study in PLOS ONE built a churn-prediction model on real banking customer data and went a step further than just predicting who would leave — it used model-interpretability techniques to explain why the model flagged each customer, turning a black-box prediction into a specific, actionable reason. That distinction matters enormously for a SaaS business: knowing a customer has an 80% chance of churning is only useful if you also know it’s because their usage of a specific core feature dropped 60% in the last billing cycle, not some generic risk score.

This is precisely the same statistical logic used in linear regression to figure out what actually predicts a numeric outcome — except here the outcome is categorical (churn or no churn), which calls for the classification version of the same idea. Instead of guessing that “engagement” matters, you take everything you can plausibly measure about account behavior and let the pattern in your own usage data rank which signals actually predict cancellation, rather than which ones simply correlate with it.

Fear #2: Net Revenue Retention Can Hide a Real Problem Behind a Reassuring Number

Here’s where SaaS metrics get genuinely deceptive, and it isn’t anyone’s fault — it’s built into how the metric is constructed. Net revenue retention (NRR) combines two very different things into one figure: revenue lost to churned and downgraded accounts, and revenue gained from expansion and upsells among customers who stayed. A company can post a comfortable 105% NRR while quietly losing a meaningful share of its logo base, as long as expansion revenue from the accounts that stayed is large enough to mask it. Industry benchmarking on this point is explicit: rising churn with flat revenue churn typically means you’re shedding small accounts while retaining large ones — a pattern that looks fine on the NRR line and terrible on the logo-count line, and the two metrics can tell completely different stories in the same quarter.

This is the SaaS version of the collinearity trap that shows up across every business with more than one channel or signal moving at once. Product usage, NPS score, support-ticket volume, and expansion revenue all tend to rise and fall together for a healthy account and fall together for an unhealthy one — which makes it tempting to treat them as one combined “health score” without checking which individual signal is actually doing the predictive work. A patent filing on marketing and customer-behavior attribution describes almost the identical failure pattern in a different setting: rising inventory and rising sales often move together because both are being driven by a third factor — anticipated demand — and treating the correlated metric as the cause leads a business to double down on the wrong lever the next time the trend reverses. In SaaS, the equivalent mistake is assuming a rising feature-adoption number is causing retention, when both might simply be downstream of a genuinely engaged customer segment that would have stayed regardless.

The practical fix mirrors what customer-experience researchers building account-level churn tools have converged on: predict and act on churn risk at the individual account level using specific behavioral signals, rather than reading retention health off one blended, top-line number.

Both Fears at Once: Forecasting Revenue Under Real Uncertainty

Every SaaS founder eventually has to answer a board question that sounds simple and isn’t: “what will ARR be next quarter?” The honest answer depends on at least three uncertain numbers at once — new bookings, churn rate, and expansion revenue — each of which moves independently and none of which is known in advance.

Douglas Hubbard’s How to Measure Anything: Finding the Value of “Intangibles” in Business makes the case that presenting a single-point forecast for a number driven by multiple uncertain inputs is one of the more common ways businesses mislead themselves and their boards — not through dishonesty, but through false precision. The fix, Monte Carlo simulation, is available in an ordinary spreadsheet: instead of guessing one ARR figure, you describe a realistic range for churn (say, 3% to 5.5%), a range for new bookings, and a range for expansion revenue, then run those ranges through thousands of randomized combinations. What comes back is a full distribution — a most-likely ARR figure, but also the odds of landing meaningfully below plan, which is usually the number that actually matters for a hiring or spending decision made this quarter. Hubbard notes this is the same basic technique used to sharpen forecasting in fields as different as space-mission risk assessment and oil exploration, built with tools no more exotic than the spreadsheet already open on a finance team’s desktop.

What to Actually Do With This on Monday Morning

  • Split your churn number by account size before you present it. A flat blended churn rate can hide a small-account bleed sitting underneath a healthy-looking NRR — check whether churn and revenue churn are telling the same story or diverging.
  • Rank your usage signals instead of averaging them into a single health score. The PLOS ONE and MLKE research both point the same direction: a handful of specific behavioral signals (login frequency, core-feature usage, support patterns) do most of the predictive work, and a blended score can bury the one that actually matters for a given account.
  • Before crediting a “healthy” metric with retention, isolate it. If NPS, usage, and expansion revenue are all rising together for your best accounts, look for the rare account where they diverge — that’s where you’ll actually learn which one is doing the causal work.
  • Turn next quarter’s ARR forecast into a range, not a point estimate. Even a rough three-scenario version — bear case, base case, bull case — gives your board (and you) a more honest and more useful number than a single confident guess.
