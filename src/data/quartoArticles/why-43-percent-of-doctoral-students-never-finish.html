The Number Nobody Tells You at Orientation

Somewhere between the acceptance letter and the first year of coursework, almost every doctoral student hears some version of “you’ve got this.” What almost nobody says out loud is what the research actually shows: a large share of the people sitting in that orientation room will not finish.

The most comprehensive data on this comes from the Council of Graduate Schools’ PhD Completion Project, which tracked roughly 49,000 students across 30 institutions and 54 disciplines. Ten years after starting, the completion rate sat at 56.6% — meaning 43.4% of doctoral students had not completed their degree. That’s not a niche finding from one struggling program. It’s a pattern replicated across decades of follow-up research, and it varies meaningfully by field: the CGS data, along with earlier work by Bowen and Rudenstine, found that students in the natural sciences complete at higher rates than students in the social sciences, while attrition in the physical and mathematical sciences ran consistently higher than in other broad fields through years seven to ten.

Here’s the question that matters more than the headline number: when do most students who leave actually leave? Attrition research consistently points to two danger zones — the transition out of coursework, and the long, often isolated stretch of independent data collection and analysis that follows it. That second zone is where Chapter 4 lives.

Why Chapter 4 Is Where the Wheels Come Off

In the standard five-chapter dissertation format used across most social science, education, and business doctoral programs, Chapter 4 is the results — or data analysis — chapter. It’s the chapter where a candidate has to take everything collected in Chapter 3’s methodology and actually produce a defensible statistical or analytical result. It’s also, structurally, the first chapter in the entire dissertation where a mistake can’t be talked around with better writing. A weak literature review can be strengthened with more sources. A weak Chapter 4 usually means the underlying analysis has to be redone.

This structural fact lines up with what attrition researchers describe as the “ABD” phenomenon — all-but-dissertation students who completed coursework and comprehensive exams but stalled during the independent research phase. A student who hits a wall in Chapter 4 isn’t failing because they don’t understand their topic. They’re often failing because nobody taught them, in any structured way, how to actually run and defend a regression model, a factor analysis, or a set of hypothesis tests under committee scrutiny. Coursework covers statistics in the abstract. Chapter 4 requires applying it, alone, to messy real data that rarely behaves the way a textbook example does.

What the Attrition Data Doesn’t Say — But Implies

The CGS study didn’t set out to measure statistical competence specifically. But its field-level findings are suggestive. Programs and fields with heavier quantitative demands — physical and mathematical sciences in particular — showed higher attrition than fields with comparatively lighter statistical requirements, even after accounting for the seven-to-ten-year completion window where most of that gap has already opened up. That doesn’t prove statistics alone drives attrition. It’s consistent, though, with the idea that a chapter requiring independent, defensible quantitative work is a genuine bottleneck, not just a formality between the proposal and the defense.

Separate research specifically evaluating statistical practice inside completed theses backs this up from a different angle. A 2025 study published in PLOS ONE reviewed 139 master’s and doctoral theses submitted to an Open, Distance and e-Learning university across seven agricultural and environmental science disciplines, and found recurring, correctable mistakes in how statistical techniques were chosen and applied — students weren’t lacking effort or intelligence, they were making specific, identifiable, and preventable choices about method selection that a second set of trained eyes would have caught before the defense, not after it.

The Part That’s Actually Good News

If attrition were purely about intelligence or work ethic, there wouldn’t be much a candidate could do differently. But the pattern in the research points somewhere more actionable: attrition concentrates at a specific, identifiable stage — the independent analysis stage — doing a specific, identifiable kind of work — applied statistics under committee scrutiny — where specific, identifiable mistakes recur across disciplines and institutions.

That means the leverage point isn’t “try harder” or “be smarter.” It’s getting the Chapter 4 analysis reviewed, structured, and defensible before it reaches a committee for the first time, rather than after a committee sends it back. A candidate who gets their regression assumptions checked, their sample size justified, and their results table built to the standard a committee expects isn’t cutting a corner — they’re addressing the exact stage where the national data says people most often stall out.

What to Actually Do About This

  • Treat Chapter 4 as its own project, with its own timeline, separate from writing the rest of the dissertation. The data suggests this stage is where candidates who’ve cleared every other hurdle still get stuck.
  • Get your statistical approach reviewed before data collection is finished, not after. Several of the recurring errors identified in the PLOS ONE review of 139 theses were method-selection mistakes made at the design stage, which are far cheaper to fix before data collection than after.
  • If your field runs the higher-attrition side of the CGS data — physical, mathematical, or other quantitatively heavy disciplines — budget extra calendar time specifically for the analysis chapter, not just for writing generally.
  • Don’t wait for a committee rejection to be your first quality check. The whole point of the attrition research is that this stage is where feedback arrives too late to be useful if you’re getting it for the first time in a committee meeting.
