The Survey That Looked Fine Until Chapter 4

A candidate builds a 30-item survey to measure something like “leadership effectiveness” or “student engagement” — a handful of items per sub-construct, borrowed and adapted from prior instruments, reviewed for face validity, piloted with a small group. It feels rigorous. It looks rigorous. Then, in Chapter 4, a factor analysis is run to confirm the survey is actually measuring the distinct sub-constructs it was designed around — and the items don’t cleanly separate. Questions meant to measure “communication” load onto the same factor as questions meant to measure “trust.” The tidy theoretical structure from Chapter 2 doesn’t show up in the actual data.

This is one of the more disorienting moments in a quantitative dissertation, because it can feel like a personal failure — as if the researcher did something wrong in building the survey. Usually, the explanation is more structural than that, and it’s directly related to the same underlying statistical issue that drives multicollinearity in regression: when survey items are too closely correlated with each other, the data genuinely can’t support treating them as separate, distinct measures, no matter how conceptually distinct they seemed on paper.

What Factor Analysis and PCA Are Actually Doing

Both techniques — Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and factor analysis — exist to solve the same basic problem: you have more variables (survey items) than you actually need, because many of them are capturing overlapping information. Rather than analyzing 30 individual items separately, these techniques look for the underlying pattern of correlation across all of them and group items that move together into a smaller number of components or factors. Done well, this confirms your survey’s structure and gives you a smaller, more interpretable set of measures to work with in later analysis. Done on the wrong data, or with the wrong sample size, it produces a confusing, unstable result that doesn’t match your theoretical framework — and that mismatch is exactly what a committee will ask about.

The Sample Size Problem Nobody Tells You About Until It’s Too Late

Unlike regression, where a sample-size rule can be calculated from the number of predictors alone, factor analysis and PCA have a sample-size requirement that depends on something less obvious: how strongly your items are actually correlated with each other in the first place. Foundational research by Guadagnoli and Velicer, published in Psychological Bulletin, examined exactly this relationship and found that the sample size needed for a stable, reproducible factor structure depends heavily on the strength of item correlations — meaning there is no single safe number, like “always use at least 300 participants,” that applies across every survey design. A survey with strongly, clearly correlated items can produce a stable factor structure with a comparatively modest sample. A survey with weakly correlated items — even one with hundreds of respondents — can still produce an unstable factor structure that shifts meaningfully if you rerun the analysis on a slightly different sample.

This has an uncomfortable but important implication for dissertation planning: a candidate who simply borrows a generic “10 participants per item” or “300 minimum” rule from a methods textbook, without checking it against their own instrument’s actual correlation structure, may be flying blind on exactly the number a committee is most likely to ask about directly.

Why the Factors Don’t Always Match the Theory

When a factor analysis produces a structure that doesn’t match the theoretical sub-constructs a survey was designed around, there are a handful of well-documented, non-catastrophic explanations, and distinguishing between them is what separates a defensible discussion section from a vague one:

  • The sub-constructs genuinely aren’t as distinct as the underlying theory assumed, at least not in the population you sampled. This isn’t a failure — it’s a legitimate, reportable finding, and dissertations have been built around exactly this kind of result.
  • Your items are wording-correlated rather than construct-correlated — several items are similarly phrased or similarly structured in a way that makes them statistically related regardless of what they’re theoretically measuring. This is a design issue worth naming explicitly in your limitations section.
  • Your sample simply wasn’t large enough, relative to your items’ correlation strength, to produce a stable structure — the Guadagnoli and Velicer finding above. A factor analysis run on too small a sample, relative to how weakly your items correlate, can produce a structure that looks meaningful but wouldn’t replicate.

What to Actually Do About This

  • Check your item inter-correlations before you run a factor analysis, not after. If most of your items correlate only weakly with each other, you likely need a larger sample than a generic rule of thumb would suggest — and knowing this before data collection ends, rather than after, is the difference between a fixable problem and a finished, underpowered dataset.
  • Report your sample size decision with the same rigor a regression chapter requires, citing your own item correlation structure rather than a borrowed generic number — this is precisely the kind of specificity that satisfies a methodologically trained committee member.
  • If your factor structure doesn’t match your theoretical framework, treat it as a finding to explain, not a failure to hide. A well-reasoned discussion of why the empirical structure diverged from theory is often stronger, and more defensible in a live defense, than forcing your data to match a structure it doesn’t actually support.
  • Distinguish, explicitly, between a construct-validity problem and a sample-size problem in your limitations section — committees respond far better to a candidate who can name which one applies than to one who treats an unstable factor structure as an unexplained anomaly.
