The Spreadsheet That Doesn’t Answer the Question You’re Actually Asking

Picture a dental practice owner, three years in, sitting down on a Sunday night with a laptop and a coffee. The practice management software has spit out a year of numbers: new patient count by month, marketing spend by channel, recall appointment rates, treatment acceptance percentages, a column for Google Ads clicks, another for Instagram engagement, a column of five-star reviews.

She scrolls through it twice. Marketing spend is up 22% this year. New patients are up too — but only 4%. Two associates left mid-year and patient recall rates dipped in the same quarter. Is that related? The reviews are glowing. So why did the last quarter feel so quiet in the waiting room?

Nothing here is technically wrong. Every number in that sheet is accurate. The problem isn’t the data. The problem is that a spreadsheet full of correct numbers can still leave you no closer to the two questions that actually keep a business owner up at night:

  1. Am I losing customers I should be keeping?
  2. Am I missing revenue I should be capturing?

Almost every “we need better data” conversation, in almost every industry, traces back to one of those two fears. This article is about a handful of ideas from statistics and financial modeling that were built to answer exactly those questions — explained without formulas, without code, and without pretending you need a data science degree to use them.

Fear #1: Why Patients (or Customers) Actually Leave — And Why Your Gut Is Only Half Right

Most practice owners have a theory about why patients drift away. Price is the usual suspect. Sometimes it’s true. Often it isn’t.

A study published in BMC Oral Health surveyed 1,121 dental patients and 77 dentists across 41 practices, asking each side, separately, what actually drove a patient’s satisfaction and loyalty. The researchers didn’t rely on opinion. They ran the responses through statistical regression — the same basic tool a bank uses to figure out what predicts a loan default, or a retailer uses to figure out what predicts a sale. What came out the other side was a ranked list of what genuinely moved the needle: how convenient the location was, the patient’s trust in the dentist’s clinical decisions, how clearly treatment was explained, whether the dentist seemed to actually know the patient’s history, and how satisfied patients were with visit frequency. Price wasn’t at the top of the list. Feeling known and informed was.

Here’s the part practice owners don’t expect: the study also compared what dentists thought mattered to patients against what patients said actually mattered — and found real gaps between the two. In other words, the dentists’ gut instincts and the patients’ real reasons for staying loyal didn’t fully line up. That gap is expensive, because it means marketing dollars and staff training time are quietly being spent on the wrong fix.

A separate peer-reviewed analysis in the Westcliff International Journal of Applied Research went further, cataloguing eighteen distinct factors that influence why a patient stays with (or leaves) a specific dental practice, then statistically ranking them by how much US patients actually cared about each one. The findings echo a pattern that shows up across almost every service business studied in the customer-retention literature, going back to one of the foundational papers in this field — Athanassopoulos’s 2000 study in the Journal of Business Research, which linked customer satisfaction directly to the specific, measurable behaviors that predict whether someone switches providers. The lesson holds well outside dentistry: retention is not a mood, it’s a short list of specific, measurable things, and you can find that list with your own data instead of guessing.

Why this matters more than it looks like it does. A wide body of published research on customer churn — including a systematic review of over sixty peer-reviewed studies in the journal Machine Learning and Knowledge Extraction, and a 2023 study in PLOS ONE that built and explained a churn-prediction model for a bank — makes the same basic point in industry after industry: once you can predict who is likely to leave, you can usually explain why, and once you know why, retaining an existing customer is dramatically cheaper than acquiring a new one. None of this requires the sophistication of a bank’s fraud team. It requires the same discipline: stop guessing which factor matters, and let the pattern in your own appointment book tell you.

Fear #2: The Marketing Dashboard Trap — When Two Numbers Move Together but Only One of Them Is Actually Working

Here is where most small business owners get quietly misled, and it isn’t their fault. It’s a well-documented pattern.

Say Google Ads spend rose in March. So did referral bookings. So did Instagram followers. It’s tempting to conclude that the ad spend caused the referral bump. But a business analysis of marketing attribution errors captured almost exactly this trap in a widely cited example: a company saw revenue drop and blamed its affiliate marketing channel, because the drop showed up right as affiliate activity increased. The real cause turned out to be a separate email promotion the company itself had launched at the same time, which was quietly pulling sales away from the very channels it appeared to be correlated with. The company had the right data and drew the wrong conclusion, because two numbers moving together is not proof that one is causing the other.

This exact trap has a name in statistics — analysts call it collinearity, when two or more things you’re tracking move together so closely that it becomes almost impossible to tell which one is actually doing the work. It isn’t a marketing-specific problem, either. Patent filings on marketing attribution systems describe the identical failure pattern showing up in classic sales forecasting: a rising inventory level and rising sales often move together, and a naive read of the numbers assumes stocking more inventory is causing the sales increase — when in reality, both are being driven by a third thing entirely, like a seasonal spike in demand that the business anticipated and stocked up for. Treat the correlated number as the cause, and you’ll double down on the wrong lever the next time sales dip.

For a dental practice, the equivalent trap looks like this: Google Ads spend, Instagram activity, and word-of-mouth referrals often rise and fall together, because they’re all responding to the same underlying thing — a new hygienist, extended hours, a location change, a seasonal back-to-school rush. Cut the Google Ads budget because “it’s not working” and you may be cutting the one channel that was never doing the heavy lifting to begin with, while the real driver survives the cut and takes the credit anyway.

The practical fix isn’t complicated. It’s the same fix professional marketers reach for once they get burned by this: stop reading channels in isolation, and instead test one variable at a time — pause a single channel for a defined period, in a defined area, and watch what actually happens to the numbers that matter, rather than trusting a chart where three lines happen to be climbing together.

Both Fears at Once: Stress-Testing a Decision Before You Make It

Here’s a question no spreadsheet answers by itself: if you hire a third hygienist, what’s the actual range of outcomes over the next twelve months? Best case, worst case, and — more usefully — the case that’s actually most likely?

Most business owners answer this with a single guess: “we’ll probably see $40,000 more in revenue.” A single number feels precise. It’s also almost always wrong, because it pretends there’s no uncertainty in patient volume, no-show rates, insurance reimbursement timing, or how long it takes a new hire to get fully booked.

Douglas Hubbard, in his widely read book How to Measure Anything: Finding the Value of Intangibles in Business, makes the case that this single-number habit is one of the most expensive mistakes in business decision-making — and that the fix doesn’t require a statistics degree, just a spreadsheet and a technique called Monte Carlo simulation. Instead of guessing one outcome, you describe a realistic range for each uncertain input — say, “new patient volume will likely land somewhere between 8 and 20 a month, with 14 being the most probable” — and then let the spreadsheet run that scenario thousands of times with slightly different random combinations of those ranges. What comes out isn’t a single confident answer. It’s a full picture: a 15% chance you lose money in year one, a 60% chance you land somewhere solidly profitable, and a 10% chance it’s a home run. That’s a very different, and far more honest, basis for a hiring decision than a single guessed number — and it’s the same basic technique Hubbard notes has been used to sharpen everything from NASA mission risk assessments to oil exploration forecasts, built with tools no more exotic than the spreadsheet you already have open.

What to Actually Do With This on Monday Morning

You don’t need a data science team to start applying any of this. You need your existing records and about an hour.

  • Pull twelve months of “patients who left” and look for the pattern, not the excuse. Don’t ask why any one patient left. Ask what the patients who left have in common — same time of day, same procedure type, same insurance, same staff member, same season — the same way the BMC Oral Health researchers isolated a ranked list of real drivers instead of accepting anecdotes.
  • Before cutting any marketing channel, isolate it. Pause it in one location or for one defined month rather than reading it off a dashboard where every line moves together. That’s the only way to tell a real driver from a bystander that happened to be correlated.
  • Turn your next big “should we spend on this” decision into a range, not a guess. Write down a realistic low, likely, and high outcome instead of one number, and think in terms of the odds of each, the way Hubbard’s Monte Carlo approach frames it.
  • Trust the ranked list over the loudest opinion in the room. The gap the BMC Oral Health study found — between what providers assumed mattered and what actually did — shows up in nearly every service business we’ve looked at. Your own numbers are a better witness than anyone’s intuition, including your own.

None of this is unique to dentistry. Swap “patient” for “client,” “policyholder,” or “subscriber” and the same two fears, and the same fixes, apply to a financial planning practice, a med-spa, or a subscription box business.
