The Study That Reviewed a Year of Studies

In 1962, a psychologist named Jacob Cohen did something almost nobody had done before. Instead of running a new experiment, he pulled every article published in a full year of the Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology and calculated, for each one, the statistical power the study actually had, meaning the real probability that the study could have detected the effect it was looking for, assuming that effect genuinely existed at a moderate size.

The number he found was startling for a field that ran on statistical significance testing. The average study in his sample had roughly a 48 percent chance of detecting a medium sized effect, worse odds than a coin flip. Researchers were running experiments, getting a null result, and concluding an effect didn’t exist, when in a huge share of cases the study had simply never had a realistic chance of finding it even if the effect was real. The problem wasn’t bad theories. It was sample sizes chosen by convention, by what was convenient to collect, or by what a previous study happened to use, rather than by any calculation of what the study actually needed to detect what it claimed to be testing.

Cohen’s answer to the problem he’d found became one of the most widely used tools in applied research: a way to calculate, before running a study, exactly how large a sample needs to be to have a reasonable chance, conventionally 80 percent, of detecting an effect of a given size. That calculation, power analysis, is the honest answer to the question almost every dissertation student eventually asks a committee member in some form: how many participants do I actually need.

Why “Ten Per Predictor” Is a Guess, Not an Answer

Ask around and you’ll hear rules of thumb passed down like folklore: ten participants per predictor variable, a hundred participants minimum, whatever the last student in the program used. Some of these rules aren’t unreasonable starting points. None of them are actually calculated for your specific study, and that gap is exactly the one Cohen’s 1962 review exposed at scale.

The real answer depends on four things that a rule of thumb can’t know: how large an effect you’re realistically expecting to find, based on prior research in your area, how many predictors are in your model, how much those predictors are likely to overlap with each other, and how much risk you’re willing to accept of missing a real effect. A study designed to detect a large, obvious effect needs meaningfully fewer participants than one designed to detect a small, subtle one, and no generic rule of thumb can tell the difference, because it was never built with your specific research question in mind.

The Cost of Getting This Wrong Doesn’t Show Up Until the End

An underpowered study doesn’t announce itself as underpowered. It runs like any other study, produces a result, and if that result happens to be non significant, it’s very easy to write it up as evidence the effect isn’t there. That’s precisely the mistake Cohen’s review caught happening across an entire field: a non significant result from a study with roughly a coin flip’s chance of detecting a real effect is close to uninterpretable, and yet it routinely got written up as if it settled the question.

For a dissertation, that mistake is expensive in a very specific way. A committee reviewing a proposal with no power justification behind the sample size can reasonably ask why the number was chosen, and “that’s what the last student used” is not an answer that survives that question. Worse, a student who collects data with too small a sample and gets a null result has no clean way to tell a reader whether the effect genuinely isn’t there or whether the study simply never had a fair chance to find it, a distinction that matters enormously for how the findings get discussed in the final chapter.

Doing the Calculation Instead of Guessing

A real power analysis doesn’t require advanced statistical training, and it’s the step that turns a guessed sample size into a defensible one. It starts with an honest estimate of the effect size you expect, ideally anchored to a prior study in your specific area rather than a generic convention, since a “medium” effect in one field can look very different from a “medium” effect in another. From there, free tools built specifically for this purpose, most commonly the software G*Power, take that expected effect size along with the number of predictors in your model and your desired power, conventionally 80 percent, and calculate the minimum sample size needed.

The output isn’t just a number to hand a committee. It’s a defense: this sample size was chosen because it gives the study a genuine, calculated chance of detecting the effect being studied, not because it was convenient or customary. That’s the exact standard Cohen’s review found missing from most of a field’s published research in 1962, and it remains the difference between a sample size that survives a committee’s questions and one that quietly hopes nobody asks.

  • Start from an expected effect size, not a target sample size. Look for the closest prior study in your specific area and use its effect size as your anchor, rather than borrowing a generic convention.
  • Run the actual power calculation before collecting data, not after. A free tool like G*Power takes minutes and turns a guessed number into a number you can defend.
  • Treat a null result as ambiguous unless the study was adequately powered. A non significant finding from an underpowered study doesn’t tell you the effect isn’t there. It tells you the study wasn’t built to find out.
