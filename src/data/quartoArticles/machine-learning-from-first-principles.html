Machine Learning From First Principles: What It Is, How It Predicts, and Why It Quietly Fails

A business owner does not need to write code to make good decisions about machine learning. What a business owner needs is an honest, complete picture of how these models actually work, where their power comes from, and where that power runs out. This article builds that picture from the ground up. We start with plain definitions. We end with the actual formulas a model uses to learn, the closed form equation that trains it, and a body of published research showing exactly how these models fool their own creators.

By the end, you will understand machine learning at a technical level most business content never reaches, without needing a statistics degree to follow along.

Start simple: three words people use interchangeably, and should not

Artificial intelligence, machine learning, and deep learning get used as synonyms in casual conversation. They are not synonyms. They describe three nested ideas, each one narrower than the last.

Artificial intelligence is the broadest goal. It covers any attempt to automate a task that normally requires human judgment. A chess program built from thousands of hand written “if this position, then that move” rules technically counts as artificial intelligence, even though the program never learns anything. It just follows instructions a person wrote in advance.

Machine learning is a specific method for reaching that goal. Instead of a person writing the rules, the system studies examples and works out the rules itself. IBM traces the origin of the term to 1959, when IBM engineer Arthur Samuel used it to describe a checkers program that improved its own play the more games it studied. That single shift, letting a system improve through experience rather than through a programmer’s instructions, is the entire idea behind machine learning.

Deep learning is one specific technique inside machine learning. It stacks many layers of pattern detection on top of each other, letting a neural network build increasingly abstract representations of raw data. Most everyday business problems, pricing, forecasting, and customer scoring, do not require deep learning at all. A much older and far simpler technique usually gets the job done, and we will spend most of this article on exactly that technique.

The formal definition, and why it matters for your data

Computer scientist Tom Mitchell offered a widely cited definition of machine learning, referenced across modern academic machine learning research. The idea, paraphrased, is that a program learns from experience if its performance on some task improves as that experience grows, measured against a specific performance metric.

This definition sounds abstract until you notice what it implies. If the experience is bad, the improvement never happens. A model trained on incomplete, outdated, or mislabeled data has effectively been given bad experience. No algorithm can fix that afterward. This single idea explains why the data pipeline behind a model usually matters more than the model itself, a theme we will return to throughout this series.

Supervised learning: teaching a model with an answer key

IBM’s overview of machine learning algorithms describes supervised learning as training toward some ground truth using a loss function, a formula that measures how wrong the model’s predictions are. Every training example comes with the correct answer attached. The model studies thousands of these paired examples and gradually adjusts itself to reduce its error.

Supervised learning splits into two jobs. Regression predicts a continuous number, such as tomorrow’s revenue or next month’s stock return. Classification predicts a category, such as whether a customer churns or a transaction is fraudulent. This article focuses on regression. A companion piece in this series covers classification in full technical depth.

Unsupervised learning: finding structure with no answer key at all

Unsupervised learning removes the answer key entirely. The model receives raw data and is asked to find structure inside it without any guidance on what a correct answer looks like. This approach powers clustering, which groups similar customers or products automatically, and dimensionality reduction, which compresses complicated datasets down to their most important underlying signals.

This matters enormously for businesses sitting on years of unstructured records with no labels attached, which describes most companies before they invest in proper data infrastructure.

The features matrix: the actual object a model learns from

Every supervised model starts with two pieces of data. The features matrix, written as X, holds your inputs. Each row is one example, such as one customer, one transaction, or one trading day. Each column is one measured feature, such as price, past return, or account age.

The labels vector, written as y, holds the correct answer for every row in X. The model itself is simply a function, written as g, that takes X as input and tries to output values as close as possible to y:

\hat{y} = g(X)

Training means searching for the specific version of g that makes this prediction as accurate as possible across your entire dataset. Once trained, that function gets applied to brand new rows of X where the true answer is not yet known. That is the entire commercial value of the exercise. A trained model converts yesterday’s labeled history into tomorrow’s prediction.

A concrete example: predicting whether a market trend continues

To make this less abstract, consider a real strategy used across financial markets called time series momentum. The rule is simple to state. Buy an asset that has recently gone up. Sell an asset that has recently gone down. The underlying bet is that recent price trends carry some predictive information about near term future returns.

This is not a hypothetical toy example. Moskowitz, Ooi, and Pedersen published a landmark study in the Journal of Financial Economics testing this exact idea across 58 different futures markets, spanning equities, currencies, commodities, and bonds, over more than twenty five years of data. Their finding was striking. A security’s own past twelve month return positively predicted its future return, and this pattern held consistently across every single asset class tested, not just a few cherry picked markets.

Here is the genuinely surprising part. When the researchers combined this signal across all 58 markets into one diversified portfolio, the resulting Sharpe ratio came out around 1.0, a measure of return earned per unit of risk taken. For comparison, the long run Sharpe ratio of the entire United States stock market sits around 0.4. A mechanical rule this simple, buy winners and sell losers, roughly matched or beat one of the most studied investment strategies in modern finance, purely through diversification across markets that were not perfectly correlated with each other.

Turning that idea into a trainable formula

Linear regression is the simplest way to turn an idea like time series momentum into a trainable model. It assumes your predicted output is a weighted sum of your input features, plus one constant term:

\hat{y} = \theta_0 + \theta_1 x_1 + \theta_2 x_2 + \dots + \theta_n x_n

Here, each x represents one input feature, such as the asset’s return over the past ten days, twenty five days, or sixty days. Each theta, written as θ, is a weight the model must learn. θ₀ is called the bias term, and it shifts every prediction up or down by a constant amount.

How the model finds its weights: minimizing squared error

Training searches for the specific values of theta that make predictions as close as possible to the real outcomes, across every row in the training data. This is done by minimizing the mean squared error:

\min_{\theta} \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^{m} \left(y^{(i)} - x^{(i)}\theta\right)^2

This formula takes the gap between each real outcome and each predicted outcome, squares that gap, and averages the squared gaps across all m training examples. Squaring the error does two things at once. It removes the effect of negative signs, and it punishes large mistakes far more severely than small ones, which pushes the model away from occasional wild predictions.

Two ways to actually solve this equation

There are two standard ways to find the weights that minimize this error. The first is a closed form solution called the Normal Equation, which solves for theta directly using matrix algebra:

\hat{\theta} = (X^T X)^{-1} X^T y

This equation gives you the exact answer in one calculation. The catch is the matrix inversion step, written as the exponent negative one, which becomes extremely expensive to compute once your dataset has many features or many rows. For small, well behaved datasets it works fine. For anything resembling real commercial scale, it does not.

The second approach, used in nearly every production machine learning system, is an iterative method called gradient descent. Instead of solving directly, the model starts with a rough guess for theta and repeatedly nudges it in the direction that reduces error the fastest:

\theta^{[\text{next step}]} = \theta^{[\text{previous step}]} - \eta \nabla_{\theta} MSE\left(\theta^{[\text{previous step}]}\right)

The Greek letter eta, η, is called the learning rate. It controls how large each step is. Scikit-learn’s documentation on ordinary least squares confirms that this general family of approaches, direct matrix solving for smaller problems and iterative gradient methods for larger ones, remains the standard toolkit for linear regression in production systems today.

Measuring whether the model is actually any good

Once trained, you need a number that tells you how well the model fits. The standard measure is the coefficient of determination, written as R squared:

R^2 = 1 - \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m} \left(y^{(i)} - x^{(i)}\hat{\theta}\right)^2}{\sum_{i=1}^{m} \left(y^{(i)} - \bar{y}\right)^2}

Here, y-bar is simply the average label value across your training sample. R squared compares your model’s prediction error against the error you would get by guessing the average every single time. An R squared close to 1.0 means the model explains almost all the variation in the data. An R squared close to zero means it barely beats a naive average.

Here is a detail almost nobody outside a statistics course knows, and it matters enormously in practice. On your training sample, R squared will always be zero or positive as long as your model includes a bias term. On a fresh test sample the model has never seen, R squared can go negative. A negative out of sample R squared means the model performed worse than simply guessing the historical average every time, despite having looked reasonably competent during training.

The uncomfortable finding hiding inside every backtest

This gap between training performance and real world performance is not a minor technical footnote. It is arguably the single most important idea in applied machine learning, and a body of published financial research has quantified exactly how often it goes wrong.

Bailey, Borwein, Lopez de Prado, and Zhu published a widely cited paper formally defining what they call the probability of backtest overfitting. Their central finding is unsettling for anyone building predictive trading or forecasting models. A strategy selection process overfits whenever the strategy that looked best during training has an expected ranking below the median once tested on fresh, unseen data. In plain language, the strategy that appeared to win during development often performs worse than an average strategy once real, new data arrives.

This connects to an even broader problem across empirical research generally. A frequently cited paper argues that a large share of published research findings across many scientific fields do not replicate once independently retested, a pattern closely related to the same statistical trap that produces backtest overfitting. Financial economists Campbell Harvey and colleagues studied this exact issue inside asset pricing research specifically, and their recommendation is genuinely striking. Because hundreds of proposed market predictors have already been tested against the same historical data, research surveying this “factor zoo” problem recommends raising the statistical significance bar from a t-statistic of 2.0, the traditional threshold, up to 3.0, precisely because testing so many ideas against the same data makes some of them look significant by chance alone.

Underfitting and overfitting: the two ways a model can be wrong

A model can fail in two opposite directions. Underfitting happens when the model is too simple to capture the real pattern in the data, producing weak predictions even on the training set itself. Overfitting happens when the model is complex enough to memorize noise specific to the training set, producing excellent training performance and poor real world performance.

Google’s own machine learning materials frame this using Occam’s Razor, the longstanding principle that simpler explanations tend to generalize better than complicated ones, even when the complicated explanation fits the observed data slightly better on paper.

Four practical fixes address overfitting directly. Gathering more training data reduces the influence of random noise on the model. Regularization constrains how large the model’s weights can grow, a technique covered in full technical depth in the next article in this series. Early stopping halts training the moment performance on a held out validation sample starts getting worse, even if training performance keeps improving. Careful hyperparameter tuning finds the specific settings that balance fit against complexity, rather than simply maximizing accuracy on whatever data happens to be in front of you.

Why the formulas are the easy part

Everything covered in this article, the features matrix, the weighted sum formula, the normal equation, gradient descent, and R squared, represents maybe a week of study for someone with a basic statistics background. The genuinely hard, expensive, and valuable part of any real machine learning project sits somewhere else entirely: building and maintaining the pipeline that keeps your features matrix accurate, current, and complete.

A regression model trained on stale competitor pricing, incomplete transaction history, or manually copied spreadsheet data will produce confident, well formatted, completely wrong predictions. This is the exact failure mode the backtest overfitting research above describes mathematically. It is also the most common reason internal data science projects quietly underperform. Reliable ETL and data integration pipelines that keep your X matrix fresh matter at least as much as the choice of algorithm sitting on top of it, and usually matter considerably more.
