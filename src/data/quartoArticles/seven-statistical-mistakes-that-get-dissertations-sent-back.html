The Paper That Found Evidence of the Future

In 2011, Cornell psychologist Daryl Bem published a paper titled “Feeling the Future” in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, one of the most respected journals in the field. The paper reported nine experiments, run over more than a decade, appearing to show that people could sense future events before they happened, that a stimulus presented after a memory test could still measurably improve performance on the test that came before it. The paper passed peer review at a top journal. Bem was a well established, credible researcher, not a fringe figure.

What made the paper so useful as a case study wasn’t that it was fraudulent. Nobody accused Bem of fabricating data. It was that the paper was built almost entirely from a set of small, individually defensible looking statistical choices that, in combination, made it far easier than it should have been to find a significant result out of noise. Researchers running replication attempts afterward couldn’t reproduce the effect. The paper became one of the most cited catalysts for the modern replication crisis in psychology, and the specific list of statistical choices critics identified in it became, almost accidentally, one of the clearest teaching examples of exactly the mistakes that quietly undermine an otherwise well intentioned analysis.

Those same mistakes show up constantly in dissertation chapters, not because students are trying to manufacture a result, but because the same small, tempting choices are available at almost every step of an analysis, and each one looks reasonable in isolation.

Running Many Tests and Reporting Only the Significant Ones

One of the most common criticisms of the Bem paper was that several of the nine experiments included multiple possible outcome measures, and the paper tended to highlight whichever measure came back significant. Run enough tests on the same data and something will cross the conventional significance threshold by chance alone, even if nothing real is happening. A dissertation chapter that runs a dozen exploratory comparisons and reports the handful that came back significant, without correcting for the number of tests run, is repeating this exact pattern.

Treating an Exploratory Finding as a Confirmatory One

Several of Bem’s experiments described analyses that appeared to have been chosen or adjusted after seeing preliminary results, then presented with the same confidence as a hypothesis specified in advance. A finding discovered while exploring the data is a legitimate, useful thing to report, but it carries fundamentally less evidential weight than a hypothesis stated before the data was collected, and a committee will reasonably ask which kind of finding they’re looking at.

Stopping Data Collection Once the Result Looks Good

Checking a result partway through data collection and stopping once significance is reached, sometimes called optional stopping, inflates the chance of a false positive in a way that isn’t visible in the final reported statistics. Critics of the Bem paper raised exactly this concern about several of the experiments. A pre-registered, fixed sample size decided before data collection begins closes off this particular route to an inflated result entirely.

A Small Sample Producing an Overconfident Claim

Several of the nine experiments in the Bem paper had samples in the range of 50 to 150 participants for effects that, if real, would be expected to be quite small, meaning the studies had limited power to detect what they claimed to find reliably. A small sample can still produce a statistically significant result, but that result carries a much wider margin of real uncertainty than the same p value from a properly powered study, a distinction we cover directly in how many participants do you actually need for your regression chapter.

Building the Narrative After Seeing the Result

Critics also pointed to language in the paper that framed the hypotheses as though they had clearly predicted the specific pattern found, when the underlying theory was vague enough to have been consistent with several different possible outcomes. Constructing a clean theoretical narrative after seeing which result came back significant, rather than before, is one of the harder mistakes to self detect, because it feels like ordinary interpretation rather than a statistical choice.

Correlated Predictors Hiding Which Variable Actually Mattered

This isn’t a criticism specific to the Bem paper, but it’s one of the most common reasons a dissertation regression chapter gets flagged. When predictors overlap heavily with each other, a model can produce a coefficient that looks meaningful while actually reflecting instability rather than a genuine, isolated effect, the exact problem documented in the classic 1967 Longley dataset and covered in why your committee keeps flagging multicollinearity.

Reporting a Point Estimate Without Acknowledging Its Uncertainty

The broader lesson the Bem episode left behind, echoed across the replication crisis literature that followed it, is that a single reported number, a mean difference, a coefficient, a correlation, is an estimate with real uncertainty around it, not a fixed fact. Presenting a result without any acknowledgment of that uncertainty, a confidence interval, an effect size with context, a discussion of the sample’s limitations, is the pattern that most consistently draws committee scrutiny, because it claims more certainty than the underlying data actually supports.

  • Correct for the number of tests you actually ran, not just the ones that came back significant. Running a dozen comparisons and reporting three is a version of the same pattern that undermined the Bem paper.
  • Decide your sample size and stopping point before you start collecting data. Checking partway through and stopping once a result looks good inflates false positives in a way that won’t show up in your final statistics.
  • Report uncertainty alongside every key estimate. A confidence interval or effect size with honest context is what separates a defensible finding from an overconfident one.

None of these mistakes require bad intentions to happen. They require exactly the kind of small, individually reasonable choices that made “Feeling the Future” one of the most cited cautionary tales in modern research.
