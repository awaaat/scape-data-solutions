It’s Not Random What Gets a Chapter 4 Sent Back

If you talk to enough dissertation chairs, a pattern emerges fast: the reasons a data analysis chapter gets sent back for revision are not, in most cases, unique or unusual. They repeat, across disciplines, across decades, across universities. This isn’t a guess — it’s been formally studied.

One of the most cited studies on this exact question, published by researcher Bruce Thompson and revisited in a follow-up paper, reviewed methodological errors specifically within Ph.D. dissertations and organized them into seven recurring analytic principles. More recent work has confirmed the same pattern persists: a 2025 PLOS ONE study reviewed 139 master’s and doctoral theses across seven disciplines and again found recurring, correctable statistical mistakes, and a separate analysis of science education master’s theses found specific, repeatable errors like running ANOVA without first testing whether the data actually met the normality assumption the test requires.

Here’s what the research says actually goes wrong, most often, translated out of the jargon.

1. Treating a p-value as the whole story

Thompson’s research identifies over-reliance on statistical significance testing as one of the most persistent errors in dissertations — using a p-value as if it single-handedly proves a finding is important, without reporting effect size (how large the difference or relationship actually is) alongside it. A result can be statistically significant and practically trivial at the same time, especially with a large sample. Committees increasingly expect both numbers side by side, not one standing in for the other.

2. Not checking whether your data can actually support the test you’re running

The science education thesis-error study found a specific, repeatable pattern: researchers running ANOVA — a test that assumes the data is roughly normally distributed — without ever checking whether that assumption held. This isn’t a technicality. If the assumption is violated badly enough, the test’s results can’t be trusted, and a committee member trained to check for this will ask the question immediately. The fix isn’t complicated — a normality check takes minutes — but skipping it is one of the most common and most avoidable errors in the literature.

3. Using a single variable when a multivariate approach is what the question actually calls for

Thompson’s analysis singles out under-use of multivariate methods — analyzing variables one at a time when the underlying research question genuinely involves several variables acting together — as a recurring weakness. Real-world outcomes, whether in education, business, or health, are rarely driven by one factor in isolation. A dissertation that tests five separate simple relationships when the question actually calls for one combined model is doing more work to arrive at a less trustworthy answer.

4. Converting a properly measured variable down to a cruder category

One of the seven principles Thompson describes involves converting variables that were measured on a continuous or interval scale down to broad categories — turning, for instance, a precise test score into “high” and “low” groups. This throws away real information and reduces statistical power. Committees trained in quantitative methods notice this immediately, because it’s usually a sign the researcher didn’t know how to analyze the variable in its original, more informative form.

5. Choosing a statistical technique that doesn’t actually match the research design

The 2025 PLOS ONE review of 139 theses found this as a central recurring issue: using a statistical technique that doesn’t align with how the data was actually collected or what the research question is actually asking. A common example is applying a test built for independent groups to data that’s actually paired or repeated-measures in structure. The fix requires understanding the design of the study before choosing the analysis — a step that’s easy to skip under deadline pressure but expensive to fix afterward.

6. Not reporting the analysis in enough detail for a reader to verify it

A widely cited paper on statistical reporting errors found during peer review notes that the statistical methods section is frequently the most poorly reported part of a manuscript — missing details on exactly what test was run, on what comparison, with what correction for multiple comparisons, if any. In a dissertation, this shows up as a results section a committee member can’t actually verify from the text alone, which invites exactly the kind of drawn-out revision cycle every candidate wants to avoid.

7. Overstating what a limited sample can actually support

The science education thesis-error study also documented cases where findings from a small, non-random sample were presented as though they generalized to a much larger population. This is one of the most common overreach errors in applied dissertations, and it’s often unintentional — a candidate genuinely believes their finding is broader than their data can support, because nobody flagged the gap between the sample and the claim before the defense.

Why These Seven Keep Showing Up

None of these are exotic errors. They’re not about not knowing statistics exists — they’re about the gap between coursework-level statistics and the applied, defend-it-under-questioning reality of a dissertation. A candidate can pass every graduate statistics course and still make several of these mistakes, because coursework rarely simulates the pressure and ambiguity of applying a technique to your own messy, real dataset for the first time, alone, with a defense date already on the calendar.

What to Actually Do About This

  • Before you run any test, write down the assumption it requires and check it explicitly — normality, independence, equal variances, whatever applies. This single habit would have caught two of the seven errors above.
  • Report effect size next to every significance test, not instead of it. Committees increasingly expect this as standard, not optional.
  • Match your statistical technique to your actual research design before you touch your data, not after you’ve already run something and are looking for a way to justify it.
  • Have someone outside your own head check your analysis section for verifiability — could a stranger reproduce what you did from what you wrote? If not, a committee member reading it will hit the same wall.
