The Company That Was Fastest at the Wrong Thing

In 2011, a design focused flash sale site called Fab.com launched and grew faster than almost any e-commerce company anyone could point to at the time. Within about eighteen months it had raised well over 300 million dollars in venture funding, built an email list in the millions, and was, by some accounts at the time, on pace to become one of the fastest growing e-commerce businesses ever measured. By 2013 the company had reportedly reached a valuation near a billion dollars, built almost entirely on a daily flash sale model: a new deal every day, delivered by email, designed to drive an immediate first purchase.

The number that mattered far more than growth rate never got the same attention. A large share of Fab’s revenue was coming from first time purchases driven by steep, limited time discounts, and a meaningful portion of the customers those emails brought in never became repeat buyers at anything close to full price. Acquiring each new customer through paid marketing and aggressive email growth was expensive, and the flash sale model depended on discounting so heavily to drive that first purchase that the economics of keeping a customer profitable over time never worked the way the growth chart suggested they did.

By mid to late 2013, the company had already tried pivoting away from pure flash sales toward a more conventional marketplace model, and it began cutting staff in waves, first in its European operations, then again in the United States, letting go of hundreds of employees within a matter of months. The billion dollar valuation didn’t survive the correction. By 2015, Fab was sold to the hardware company PCH International for a figure reported to be a small fraction of what investors had put into it. What had briefly been called the fastest growing e-commerce company ever became one of the more frequently cited cautionary stories in startup circles about growth built on the wrong metric.

Fab didn’t fail because people stopped wanting design products delivered by email. It failed for three specific, unglamorous reasons that show up in a much smaller, much less dramatic form in an ordinary online retailer’s dashboard every week. The company chased the first sale so hard that it never properly accounted for what happened to the relationship afterward. Marketing channels that moved together got read as proof the growth engine was working, when the real driver was something else entirely. And inventory and spending decisions kept getting made on a single confident growth number instead of an honest range of outcomes.

The Cart You’re Trying to Recover Might Not Be the Real Loss

Cart abandonment gets treated as the headline metric in e-commerce, and for good reason. Research aggregated by the Baymard Institute across dozens of independent studies puts the average abandonment rate at just over 70 percent, meaning roughly seven out of every ten shoppers who add something to a cart never complete the purchase. That’s real money, one industry estimate puts the value of merchandise abandoned in online carts at close to 4 trillion dollars a year globally. Fab’s entire growth strategy was, in effect, an aggressive answer to that number: discount hard enough, urgently enough, and often enough that abandonment stopped being the problem, at least for the first purchase.

But here’s what the abandonment number alone hides, and what Fab’s later numbers eventually revealed. A peer reviewed study published in the Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science examined why shoppers actually abandon carts and found something counterintuitive: for a meaningful share of shoppers, especially those who also shop at a retailer’s physical store, the cart isn’t abandonment at all, it’s being used as a shopping list. Those lost carts sometimes convert later, offline, in a way that never shows up in the online abandonment number. Treating every abandoned cart as a fire to put out with a discount code, the way Fab’s daily flash sale model essentially did at scale, can mean training shoppers to expect a coupon for behavior they were always going to complete anyway, which quietly erodes margin without fixing anything, and eventually stops working entirely once the discount becomes the entire relationship.

The real fear underneath cart abandonment isn’t the cart. It’s what a purely acquisition focused strategy does to the relationship over time, which is exactly the number Fab’s growth chart never captured. Research from Bain and Company, cited widely across the retention literature, found that increasing customer retention by just 5 percent can increase profits by 25 to 95 percent, depending on the industry, a gap so large it tells you retention isn’t a nice to have metric sitting next to revenue, it’s one of the biggest levers on profit a retailer has. Harvard Business Review has separately reported that acquiring a new customer typically costs five to twenty five times more than keeping an existing one. Read those two findings together and the priority becomes clear, and it’s the exact priority Fab’s business model inverted: a returning customer who almost didn’t complete a purchase is worth far more attention than a brand new visitor, because the cost of losing them compounds every single order they would have placed afterward, a concept researchers call customer lifetime value, formalized in a foundational 2000 study in the Journal of Marketing by Reinartz and Kumar, which linked how long a customer stays to how profitable that customer turns out to be, not just how big any single order is.

When Every Marketing Channel Moves Together, You Can’t Tell Which One Is Working

This is the trap that cost Fab real money at scale, and it isn’t a failure of effort, it’s a well documented statistical blind spot. Say email driven flash sale revenue rises in a given month. So does paid social spend, so does organic search interest in the brand, so does the size of the email list itself. All four numbers climb together during a period of aggressive growth, because they’re all riding the same wave of marketing spend and press attention. Look at a dashboard where four lines are all pointing up and it’s tempting to credit whichever channel got the most budget, or to conclude the whole model is working. That instinct is exactly the trap analysts call collinearity, when two or more things being tracked move together so closely that it becomes almost impossible to tell which one is actually doing the work, or whether any of them reflect something sustainable.

A widely cited example from the marketing attribution literature makes this concrete in a smaller, clearer setting: a business saw a steep revenue decline and blamed its affiliate marketing channel, since the drop coincided with rising affiliate activity. The real cause was a separate email promotion the company itself had launched at the same time, which was quietly stealing conversions from the very channel it appeared to correlate with. The business had accurate data and still drew the wrong conclusion, because two numbers moving in the same direction is not proof that one is causing the other. Fab’s growth metrics moved together for a similar reason, a huge, coordinated marketing push was lifting every visible number at once, which made it extremely difficult for anyone reading the dashboard to isolate whether repeat customers, not just first time buyers pulled in by a steep discount, were actually growing the business.

Retailers who’ve been burned by this tend to land on the same fix: stop trusting overlapping lines on one chart, and instead isolate a single channel, pause it for a defined window in a defined region, and measure what actually happens to orders, not what a dashboard implies. Reported conversion rates between channels can differ by five times or more once they’re actually isolated and tested individually rather than read off a blended chart, because a channel’s credit in a shared dashboard often has more to do with when it fires in the customer journey than with whether it caused the sale at all.

Betting Inventory on a Single Number Instead of a Range

Every retailer eventually faces a version of the same decision: how much inventory to buy, and how much ad budget to commit, before a big sales period, based on a demand forecast that is really a guess dressed up as a number. Fab’s growth strategy ran on an extreme version of this same false precision, committing to aggressive marketing spend and rapid international expansion based on a single, confident extrapolation of its early growth curve, without ever pricing in the real range of outcomes if repeat purchase rates didn’t hold up once the discounts stopped driving so much of the volume.

Statistician and author Douglas Hubbard, in How to Measure Anything: Finding the Value of Intangibles in Business, argues that treating a forecast as a single number is one of the more expensive habits in business planning, and that the fix is a technique called Monte Carlo simulation, available in an ordinary spreadsheet. Instead of committing to we’ll sell 2,000 units, a retailer describes a realistic range: maybe 1,200 units on the low end, 3,500 on the high end, with 2,000 as the most likely middle. The spreadsheet then runs that range through thousands of randomized combinations and shows a full distribution of outcomes, including, critically, the odds of being stuck with expensive dead stock versus the odds of selling out and leaving revenue on the table. That’s a fundamentally more honest basis for a six figure inventory order, or a multimillion dollar marketing commitment, than a single guessed number, and it’s the same basic method Hubbard notes has sharpened forecasting in fields as different as NASA mission planning and oil exploration.

What Fab’s Collapse Actually Teaches

Fab did not fail because customers stopped wanting to buy design products online, or because a competitor out executed it. It failed because a company with real purchase data sitting in its own systems kept optimizing for the number that was easy to grow, first time purchases driven by steep discounts, while the number that actually determined whether the business worked, repeat purchase behavior at sustainable margins, went undertracked relative to how much attention it deserved. No single decision caused the collapse. Years of chasing acquisition over retention, a dashboard where every marketing metric moved together during a coordinated growth push, and inventory and spending decisions built on a single optimistic number, compounding over about three years, did.

None of that is an e-commerce specific problem. It’s what happens to any retail business that has a growth metric simple enough to put in a funding pitch and a real retention story that’s harder to see and harder to fix. The version that shows up in an ordinary online retailer’s dashboard is smaller and slower moving than Fab’s collapse, but it runs on exactly the same mechanism, and it stays invisible for exactly the same reason, because the easy number keeps looking fine right up until it doesn’t.

  • Before you discount every abandoned cart, segment it. Separate shoppers who also have a physical store or repeat purchase history from first time visitors. The Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science research suggests a chunk of your lost carts from repeat shoppers may convert without a discount at all, and training them to expect one, the way Fab’s daily flash sale model did at scale, quietly erodes margin over time.
  • Isolate one channel before you cut it, and before you credit it. Pause it in one region or for one defined window instead of reading it off a dashboard where every line moves together, that’s the only real way to separate a driver from a bystander.
  • Track retention next to revenue, not underneath it. Bain’s finding, that a 5 point retention improvement can move profit by 25 to 95 percent, means a returning customer deserves at least as much attention on your dashboard as your acquisition funnel, not less.
  • Turn your next inventory or ad spend commitment into a range, not a guess. Write down a realistic low, likely, and high outcome, and think about the odds of each, the way Hubbard’s Monte Carlo approach frames it, rather than betting the business on a single extrapolated growth line the way Fab did.

If any of this sounds like a discipline problem rather than a data problem, that’s usually because it is. Fab didn’t need a faster growth number to survive. It needed to measure the retention number its growth chart was quietly hiding, on a timeline that mattered.
