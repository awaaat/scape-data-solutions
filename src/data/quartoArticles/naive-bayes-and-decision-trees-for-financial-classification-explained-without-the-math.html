The Essay That Revived a Method Everyone Thought Was Too Simple

In August 2002, programmer and essayist Paul Graham published a short piece called “A Plan for Spam,” describing a filter he’d built to catch spam email using a statistical method called naive Bayes. At the time, most spam filtering relied on hand built rule lists, block any email containing this word, flag anything from this sender. Graham’s filter worked differently: it learned, from a set of email a person had already labeled as spam or not spam, which specific words showed up disproportionately often in each category, then used that pattern to classify new, unseen email automatically.

The method wasn’t new. Naive Bayes had existed in statistics and early computing research for decades, and many experts considered it too crude to take seriously for a real problem, because of an assumption baked into how it works that sounds almost too simple to be useful: it treats every individual piece of evidence, every word in an email, as if it were completely independent of every other word, ignoring any relationship between them entirely. Graham’s essay demonstrated something that surprised a lot of people, that this obviously wrong simplifying assumption barely hurt the filter’s real world accuracy at all, and the method he described went on to become one of the standard approaches used across the spam filtering industry for years afterward.

That same core method, along with a more literal alternative called a decision tree, is exactly what shows up constantly in financial classification today: sorting a transaction into fraud or not fraud, a loan applicant into approve or decline, an insurance claim into standard processing or manual review.

Why “Naive” Works Better Than It Has Any Right To

The “naive” part of naive Bayes refers to that independence assumption, treating every piece of evidence as unrelated to every other piece. In a spam filter, that means treating the appearance of the word “free” in an email as completely unrelated to the appearance of the word “prize,” even though in the real world those words obviously tend to show up together far more than chance alone would suggest. The assumption is technically false in almost every real application, and the method works well anyway, for a reason that turns out to matter more than getting every underlying assumption exactly right: the method only needs to get the final ranking correct, which category is most likely, not the precise, mathematically exact probability of each individual word combination. Even with a flawed independence assumption baked in, the method tends to still rank the right category as most likely far more often than not, which is the only thing that actually matters for a working classifier.

In finance, the same logic applies directly to fraud detection. A transaction happening at 3 a.m., in a country the cardholder has never visited, for an unusually large amount, are three pieces of evidence that obviously aren’t fully independent of each other in the real world. A naive Bayes model treats them as if they were anyway, multiplies together how unusual each one is on its own, and still, in practice, tends to correctly flag genuinely suspicious transactions far more often than not, precisely because the technique only needs the final ranking right, not a perfectly accurate model of how those factors truly interact.

Decision Trees: The More Literal, More Explainable Alternative

A decision tree takes a different approach to the same kind of problem, and it maps much more directly onto how a human underwriter or fraud analyst actually thinks. Instead of multiplying probabilities together behind the scenes, a decision tree asks a sequence of yes or no questions, one at a time, each one narrowing down the possibilities: is the transaction amount over a certain threshold? If yes, is it happening in a country the cardholder has transacted in before? If no, has this cardholder had a similar pattern flagged before? Each branch leads to another question, until the tree arrives at a final classification.

The appeal of a decision tree in a financial setting isn’t necessarily that it’s more accurate than naive Bayes, sometimes it is, sometimes it isn’t, depending on the specific problem. It’s that a decision tree can be read directly, question by question, by a person who isn’t a statistician, in a way that maps naturally onto an actual fraud investigation or credit decision process. A regulator or a customer asking why a specific transaction was flagged can be shown the exact sequence of questions the model followed to reach that conclusion, a level of transparency that matters enormously in a regulated industry where a decision has to be explainable, not just accurate.

Choosing Between Them, or Using Both

Neither method is strictly better across every financial classification problem, and in practice, many real systems use both together, or use an ensemble of many decision trees combined, a method called a random forest, to capture more of the real world complexity a single tree or a single naive Bayes model might miss on its own. Naive Bayes tends to work well and stays fast even with a large number of input signals, which suits high volume problems like flagging transactions in real time across millions of accounts. Decision trees tend to be preferred when explainability is the priority, when a specific decision needs to be justified clearly to a customer, an auditor, or a regulator, in plain language rather than as a probability calculation happening behind the scenes.

The lesson underneath Graham’s 2002 essay still holds two decades later: a method built on a simplifying assumption that sounds almost too crude to trust can still outperform far more elaborate approaches, as long as it’s solving the actual problem in front of it, correctly ranking which outcome is most likely, rather than trying to perfectly model every real world relationship between every piece of evidence.

  • Reach for naive Bayes when you need speed and volume. It handles a large number of input signals efficiently and remains fast enough for real time transaction screening across millions of accounts.
  • Reach for a decision tree when you need to explain the decision. Its question by question structure can be shown directly to a regulator, an auditor, or a customer in plain language.
  • Consider combining several trees into an ensemble when accuracy matters more than a single model’s simplicity. A random forest, built from many individual decision trees, often captures patterns a single tree or a single naive Bayes model misses on its own.
