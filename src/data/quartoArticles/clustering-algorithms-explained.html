Most business data arrives with no labels attached to it. Nobody tags each customer as “high value” before a sale happens. Nobody marks a transaction as fraud before an investigator looks at it. Nobody sorts a warehouse of medical scans by tissue type before a radiologist opens the file. Clustering is the branch of machine learning built for exactly this situation. It searches raw, unlabeled data and finds the groups hiding inside it, with no answer key and no human supervision guiding the process.

This article works through every major clustering method used in production systems today, using the same real numbers a data team would actually compute by hand before trusting a machine to do it. Every formula below is followed by an actual worked calculation, not just the equation on its own. At the end, you can open the companion Clustering Playground and run these exact formulas yourself, on your own numbers, in real time.

What Is Clustering in Machine Learning? What Clustering Actually Solves

Clustering takes a pile of unlabeled data points and sorts them into groups automatically. Points inside the same group should look similar to each other. Points in different groups should look clearly different. IBM’s overview of unsupervised learning describes this entire category of methods as learning structure from data that carries no predefined labels or categories at all.

Consider a subscription box company with 40,000 customers and no existing tiers — exactly the kind of problem customer segmentation and analytics projects are built to solve. A retailer with a loyalty program nobody designed on purpose. A bank whose fraud team currently reviews every single transaction by hand. Each of these businesses is sitting on a features matrix with no labels column, and clustering is the tool that reads that matrix and hands back a sensible grouping.

Hierarchical vs. Partitional Clustering: The Two Big Families

Nearly every clustering algorithm in use today falls into one of two families. Hierarchical methods build clusters step by step, using groups formed earlier as the raw material for the next step. Partitional methods decide on every cluster at once, in a single coordinated pass over the whole dataset.

Hierarchical methods split further into two directions. Agglomerative clustering starts by treating every single data point as its own miniature cluster, then repeatedly merges the two closest clusters together until one large cluster remains. Divisive clustering runs the opposite way: it starts with everything in one giant cluster and splits it apart, level by level, until every point stands alone.

Partitional methods work on a different principle entirely. A business first chooses a number of groups, then the algorithm assigns every data point to one of those groups and keeps refining the assignment until it stabilizes. K-means and k-medoids are the two most common partitional algorithms, and both are covered in full below.

Manhattan Distance vs. Euclidean Distance: Measuring Distance in Clustering

Before any algorithm can group two customers, two transactions, or two products together, it needs an exact way to measure how far apart they are. Two distance formulas dominate almost every clustering method used in practice, and switching between them can genuinely change which points end up in the same group.

Manhattan distance adds up the distance traveled along each variable separately, the way a taxi moves through city blocks rather than cutting diagonally across them:

d=∑i=1n∣Xi−Yi∣d = \sum_{i=1}^{n} |X_i - Y_i|d=i=1∑n​∣Xi​−Yi​∣

Euclidean distance measures the straight line between two points, “as the crow flies”:

d=∑j=1n(xj−yj)2d = \sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{n} (x_j - y_j)^2}d=j=1∑n​(xj​−yj​)2​

Worked Example: Comparing Two Customers

Take two real customers, measured on two variables: age and monthly spend.

  • Customer A: age 34, monthly spend $220
  • Customer B: age 51, monthly spend $340

The age gap is |34 − 51| = 17. The spend gap is |220 − 340| = 120.

Manhattan distance = 17 + 120 = 137

Euclidean distance = √(17² + 120²) = √(289 + 14,400) = √14,689 ≈ 121.2

Notice that Manhattan distance always comes out larger, since it never lets the two gaps offset each other diagonally. In practice, Manhattan distance is preferred whenever your variables represent genuinely separate, non-interchangeable steps, such as warehouse grid movement or ordinal rating scales. Euclidean distance is preferred whenever the underlying space is continuous and a diagonal shortcut is meaningful, which is why it is the default choice across most published clustering research.

You can drag both customers yourself in the Distance Duel tab of the Clustering Playground below and watch both formulas recompute live.

from scipy.spatial import distance

customer_a = (34, 220)   # age, monthly spend
customer_b = (51, 340)

manhattan = distance.cityblock(customer_a, customer_b)
euclidean = distance.euclidean(customer_a, customer_b)

print(manhattan)   # 137.0
print(euclidean)   # 121.20...

Hierarchical Clustering Explained: Agglomerative Clustering, Dendrograms, and Linkage Rules

Agglomerative clustering builds a tree structure, called a dendrogram, one merge at a time. At every step, the algorithm needs a rule for comparing two entire clusters, not just two individual points, since after the first merge you are comparing groups that already contain several members.

The average linkage rule takes the mean distance across every possible pair of points between two clusters:

1card(A) card(B)∑x∈A∑y∈Bd(x,y)\frac{1}{\text{card}(A)\,\text{card}(B)} \sum_{x \in A} \sum_{y \in B} d(x, y)card(A)card(B)1​x∈A∑​y∈B∑​d(x,y)

Here, card(A) and card(B) are simply the number of points in each cluster.

Worked Example: Merging Two Regional Sales Teams

Suppose a company is deciding whether to merge two regional customer segments, based on age and spend.

  • Segment A contains two customers: (34, 220) and (30, 200)
  • Segment B contains two customers: (60, 400) and (65, 420)

There are four cross-pairs to measure, using Euclidean distance for each:

  • (34,220) to (60,400): √(26² + 180²) = √(676 + 32,400) = √33,076 ≈ 181.9
  • (34,220) to (65,420): √(31² + 200²) = √(961 + 40,000) = √40,961 ≈ 202.4
  • (30,200) to (60,400): √(30² + 200²) = √(900 + 40,000) = √40,900 ≈ 202.2
  • (30,200) to (65,420): √(35² + 220²) = √(1,225 + 48,400) = √49,625 ≈ 222.8

Average linkage distance = (181.9 + 202.4 + 202.2 + 222.8) ÷ 4 = 809.3 ÷ 4 ≈ 202.3

A company would compare this number against every other pair of candidate segments and merge whichever pair has the smallest average linkage distance first. The other two common rules are complete linkage, which uses only the largest of the four pair distances above (222.8), and single linkage, which uses only the smallest (181.9). Each rule can produce a genuinely different final tree from the exact same starting data.

The real limitation of hierarchical clustering is permanence. Once two clusters merge, the algorithm can never undo it later. A single bad early merge, caused by two segments that looked similar on limited data, stays locked into every result downstream.

from scipy.cluster.hierarchy import linkage, fcluster
import numpy as np

points = np.array([
    [34, 220], [30, 200],   # Segment A
    [60, 400], [65, 420],   # Segment B
])

# method="average" reproduces the average-linkage distance calculated above
Z = linkage(points, method="average", metric="euclidean")
clusters = fcluster(Z, t=2, criterion="maxclust")

print(Z)          # merge history, including the 202.3-ish average linkage distance
print(clusters)   # [1 1 2 2] once both segments are cut into 2 clusters

K-Means Clustering Algorithm and K-Medoids Clustering Explained

Partitional algorithms fix a number of groups upfront, then iteratively reassign every point until the assignment stops changing.

K-Means: Building Loyalty Tiers From Scratch

IBM’s explainer on k-means clustering describes why this is the most widely used clustering algorithm in commercial machine learning: it is simple, computationally fast, and scales easily to very large datasets. Each point is assigned to whichever centroid, or cluster average, sits nearest to it.

The process runs in a loop: choose k, place k starting centroids, assign every point to its nearest centroid, then move each centroid to the average position of the points now assigned to it. Repeat until nothing moves.

Worked Example: One Full Iteration, By Hand

Take six customers, measured by age and monthly spend, and an initial guess of k = 2:

Customer Age Spend
P1 25 150
P2 28 180
P3 30 160
P4 55 500
P5 60 520
P6 58 480

Start with two centroids at P1 (25, 150) and P5 (60, 520).

Computing Euclidean distance from every point to both centroids shows P1, P2, and P3 sit closer to (25, 150), while P4, P5, and P6 sit closer to (60, 520). For example, P4 is 351.3 units from the first centroid and only 20.6 units from the second, so it joins the second group.

With the assignment settled, each new centroid becomes the average of its own members:

New Centroid 1 = average of P1, P2, P3 = ((25+28+30)/3, (150+180+160)/3) = (27.7, 163.3)

New Centroid 2 = average of P4, P5, P6 = ((55+60+58)/3, (500+520+480)/3) = (57.7, 500.0)

That single pass is one full iteration of k-means. The algorithm repeats this exact assign-then-average cycle until the centroids stop moving between rounds, at which point the tiers are considered final. You can build this exact scenario yourself in the K-Means Trainer tab below, choosing k, stepping through each iteration one click at a time, and watching the total error fall as the tiers settle.

from sklearn.cluster import KMeans
import numpy as np

customers = np.array([
    [25, 150], [28, 180], [30, 160],   # P1, P2, P3
    [55, 500], [60, 520], [58, 480],   # P4, P5, P6
])

# n_init=1 and this init mirrors the by-hand pass: start at P1 and P5
model = KMeans(n_clusters=2, init=np.array([[25, 150], [60, 520]]), n_init=1, max_iter=1)
model.fit(customers)

print(model.cluster_centers_)
# [[ 27.67 163.33]
#  [ 57.67 500.  ]]   -- matches the hand-computed centroids above

print(model.labels_)
print(model.inertia_)   # total squared distance to centroid, used later for the elbow method

K-Medoids: The Version That Resists Outliers

K-means has one well documented weakness: because a centroid is a computed average, a single extreme outlier can drag it far off course. K-medoids solves this by anchoring each cluster to one of its own real data points, called a medoid, instead of a mathematical average. Because a medoid is an actual observed customer rather than a blended average, it barely moves even when a single unusual data point joins the group. PAM, short for Partitioning Around Medoids, was one of the first algorithms built this way, and it remains the standard choice whenever a dataset contains meaningful noise, such as a handful of unusually large one-time purchases sitting inside otherwise ordinary spending data. Teams evaluating which clustering approach fits messy, real-world data often start with a data engineering and ETL audit before choosing between k-means and k-medoids.

# pip install scikit-learn-extra
from sklearn_extra.cluster import KMedoids
import numpy as np

customers = np.array([
    [25, 150], [28, 180], [30, 160],
    [55, 500], [60, 520], [58, 480],
    [500, 9000],   # one extreme outlier: a single huge one-time purchase
])

kmedoids = KMedoids(n_clusters=2, method="pam", random_state=0)
kmedoids.fit(customers)

print(kmedoids.cluster_centers_)   # real observed customers, not blended averages
print(kmedoids.labels_)
# the outlier gets absorbed into whichever cluster it's nearest to,
# without dragging that cluster's center off the real customers in it

DBSCAN and Density-Based Clustering: Anomaly Detection and Fraud Clustering

K-means and k-medoids both assume clusters look roughly like round blobs. Real fraud patterns, geographic clusters, and behavioral segments frequently do not. Density-based clustering was built specifically to find clusters of any shape, while also identifying the points that do not belong to any group at all.

Ester, Kriegel, Sander, and Xu introduced DBSCAN in 1996, and it remains one of the most cited clustering algorithms in computer science, having won the Test of Time Award at the SIGKDD data mining conference in 2014. DBSCAN needs two settings: Eps, the radius that defines a point’s neighborhood, and MinPts, the minimum number of neighbors a point needs inside that radius to count as dense.

Every point gets classified as one of three types. A core point has at least MinPts neighbors within Eps, including itself. A border point falls within Eps of a core point but does not have enough neighbors of its own to qualify as core. A noise point belongs to neither category, and gets flagged as an outlier.

Worked Example: Spotting a Fraudulent Transaction

Take five transactions, plotted on two simplified variables, with Eps set to 3:

Transaction Feature X Feature Y
T1 10 10
T2 11 11
T3 9 10
T4 10 9
T5 80 80

Checking distances from T1 to the others: T1 to T2 is √(1+1) ≈ 1.4, T1 to T3 is √(1+0) = 1.0, and T1 to T4 is √(0+1) = 1.0. All three sit inside the Eps radius of 3. With MinPts set to 3, T1 qualifies as a core point, since it has three neighbors within range. T2, T3, and T4 join the same cluster as border or core points, depending on their own neighbor counts.

T5, at (80, 80), sits far outside the Eps radius of every other transaction. It has zero neighbors within range, so DBSCAN labels it as noise, the exact signal a fraud analyst would want flagged for manual review. Try this yourself in the Fraud Detective tab, where dragging the Eps and MinPts sliders reclassifies every transaction live and counts how many get flagged.

A close relative, the SNN algorithm, uses the same core-border-noise framework but measures similarity by counting how many nearest neighbors two points share, rather than raw distance, which makes it more forgiving of small changes in scale across variables.

from sklearn.cluster import DBSCAN
import numpy as np

transactions = np.array([
    [10, 10], [11, 11], [9, 10], [10, 9],   # T1-T4, tight cluster
    [80, 80],                                # T5, the fraud outlier
])

db = DBSCAN(eps=3, min_samples=3)
db.fit(transactions)

print(db.labels_)
# [0 0 0 0 -1]  -- T1 through T4 join cluster 0, T5 is labeled -1: noise

core_mask = np.zeros_like(db.labels_, dtype=bool)
core_mask[db.core_sample_indices_] = True
print(core_mask)   # exactly which points DBSCAN classified as core points

Grid-Based Clustering (STING, CLIQUE) and Model-Based Clustering (Gaussian Mixture Models)

Grid-based clustering takes a different approach entirely. It divides the entire data space into a fixed grid of cells, computes the density of each cell, discards cells below a chosen threshold, and merges the remaining dense, neighboring cells into final clusters. STING and CLIQUE are the best known algorithms built this way. The advantage is speed: because the algorithm only compares cells, not every individual point against every other point, it scales efficiently even on very large datasets. The tradeoff is that every cluster boundary comes out strictly horizontal or vertical, since a rectangular grid can never draw a diagonal edge.

Model-based clustering assumes your data was actually generated by a mixture of underlying probability distributions layered on top of one another, typically several bell-curve, or Gaussian, distributions. Instead of hard-assigning a point to exactly one group, it calculates the probability that a point belongs to each cluster. The overall mixture density is:

f(x)=∑k=1GTkfk(x)f(x) = \sum_{k=1}^{G} T_k f_k(x)f(x)=k=1∑G​Tk​fk​(x)

where fk is the probability density function of group k, and Tk is the probability that any observation comes from that specific component.

Worked Example: Sorting Two Customer Segments by Session Length

Suppose a website has two visitor segments based on average session length in minutes. Segment A (quick browsers) has a mean of 10 minutes and a variance of 4, making up 60% of traffic. Segment B (deep researchers) has a mean of 30 minutes and a variance of 9, making up the remaining 40%.

For a univariate Gaussian, the density formula simplifies to:

fk(x)=12πσ2exp⁡(−(x−μ)22σ2)f_k(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \exp\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)fk​(x)=2πσ2​1​exp(−2σ2(x−μ)2​)

For a visitor with a 12-minute session, checking against Segment A: the exponent is −(12−10)²/(2×4) = −0.5, giving exp(−0.5) ≈ 0.607, multiplied by the coefficient 1/√(2π×4) ≈ 0.199, for a density of about 0.121.

Checking the same visitor against Segment B: the exponent is −(12−30)²/(2×9) = −18, and exp(−18) is effectively zero.

Weighting each by its share of traffic: 0.6 × 0.121 + 0.4 × 0 ≈ 0.073, driven almost entirely by Segment A. That visitor is placed in the quick-browser segment with very high confidence, purely from the math, with no manual rule ever written by a human.

from sklearn.mixture import GaussianMixture
import numpy as np

# simulate session-length data matching the two segments described above
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
segment_a = rng.normal(loc=10, scale=2, size=600).reshape(-1, 1)   # mean 10, var 4, 60%
segment_b = rng.normal(loc=30, scale=3, size=400).reshape(-1, 1)   # mean 30, var 9, 40%
sessions = np.vstack([segment_a, segment_b])

gmm = GaussianMixture(n_components=2, random_state=0)
gmm.fit(sessions)

new_visitor = np.array([[12]])   # the 12-minute session from the worked example
probs = gmm.predict_proba(new_visitor)

print(gmm.means_.ravel())        # recovers roughly [10, 30]
print(gmm.weights_)              # recovers roughly [0.6, 0.4]
print(probs)                     # near-certain probability of belonging to Segment A

How to Choose the Number of Clusters (k): Elbow Method and Rule of Thumb

Most clustering algorithms need a number, k, specified before they can run at all. Choosing it wrong either merges genuinely different groups together or splits one real group into meaningless fragments.

A fast starting estimate comes from a simple rule of thumb:

k≈n2k \approx \sqrt{\frac{n}{2}}k≈2n​​

Worked Example: Sizing a Segmentation Project

A retailer with 5,000 customers wants a rough starting point for how many segments to test. Using the rule of thumb: k ≈ √(5,000 ÷ 2) = √2,500 = 50. That is not a final answer, but it gives an analyst a sensible upper bound before running anything more rigorous.

The more common approach in practice is the elbow method. A team runs the clustering algorithm once for every candidate value of k, and tracks the total squared distance from every point to its own centroid, called inertia. Early increases in k usually cut inertia sharply. At some point, the improvement flattens into a visible bend, or elbow, and that bend marks the point where adding another cluster stops paying for itself.

Take the six-customer example from earlier. With k = 1, every point is compared against a single overall centroid at roughly (42.7, 331.7), producing a large total error. With k = 2, using the two centroids computed above, (27.7, 163.3) and (57.7, 500.0), the total error drops dramatically, since every point is now measured against a much closer centroid. That drop from k = 1 to k = 2 is the elbow effect in miniature. Testing this across many values of k by hand gets tedious fast, which is exactly what the Elbow Challenge tab below does for you instantly, letting you slide k and watch the real inertia curve form.

from sklearn.cluster import KMeans
import numpy as np

customers = np.array([
    [25, 150], [28, 180], [30, 160],
    [55, 500], [60, 520], [58, 480],
])

inertias = []
for k in range(1, 6):
    model = KMeans(n_clusters=k, n_init=10, random_state=0)
    model.fit(customers)
    inertias.append(model.inertia_)

print(inertias)
# a big drop from k=1 to k=2, then diminishing returns after -- the elbow itself

import math
n = 5000
rule_of_thumb_k = math.sqrt(n / 2)
print(round(rule_of_thumb_k))   # 50, the same starting estimate as in the worked example

Real-World Clustering Applications in Marketing, Finance, Insurance, and Fraud Detection

Marketing teams split entire customer databases into groups sharing buying behavior, then design a distinct campaign for each group. Financial teams use clustering to study currency movement, manage portfolio risk, and support credit rating decisions. Insurance companies use it to spot policyholders with unusually high claim costs, often surfacing fraud rings a rules-based system would miss. Biology researchers classify organisms by shared physical traits. City planners group neighborhoods by housing type and value. Seismologists cluster earthquake epicenters to map genuinely dangerous zones. Search platforms cluster web activity logs to detect shared browsing patterns across millions of sessions.

The Caveat Every Team Eventually Learns

Clustering will always produce an answer, whether or not real structure exists in your data. Feed it pure noise, and it will still hand back tidy, confident-looking groups. Running a cluster analysis at all means implicitly hypothesizing that meaningful groups exist in the first place, and that assumption can be false or weak. Clusters are only meaningful with respect to the specific variables used to build them, never as some universal truth about the underlying customers or transactions.

This connects to the same lesson our previous article covered from the angle of regression and financial backtesting. A model, clustering or otherwise, is only as trustworthy as the data pipeline feeding it. Reliable data engineering and ETL pipelines that keep your feature set accurate matter just as much here as they do for any predictive model.

Clustering Algorithms FAQ

What is the difference between k-means and hierarchical clustering?

K-means requires you to pick the number of clusters, k, in advance, and produces one flat set of groups. Hierarchical clustering builds a full tree of nested groups, called a dendrogram, and you decide how many clusters to keep afterward by cutting the tree at a chosen height. K-means scales to much larger datasets; hierarchical clustering gives you a complete picture of how groups nest inside each other.

What is the difference between k-means and DBSCAN?

K-means assumes clusters are roughly round and forces every point into a group, even outliers. DBSCAN makes no assumption about cluster shape and explicitly labels points that do not fit any group as noise. DBSCAN also does not require you to specify the number of clusters ahead of time, only a neighborhood radius (Eps) and a minimum point count (MinPts).

How does DBSCAN clustering work?

DBSCAN classifies every point as core, border, or noise based on how many neighbors fall within a set radius, Eps. A point with at least MinPts neighbors within Eps becomes a core point, and clusters grow outward from core points. Points that fall within range of a core point but lack enough neighbors of their own become border points, and everything else is flagged as noise. You can watch this classification happen live in the Fraud Detective tab of the playground above.

How do you choose the right number of clusters?

Two common approaches: a fast rule-of-thumb estimate, k ≈ √(n/2), where n is the number of data points, and the elbow method, which runs the algorithm across a range of k values and looks for the point where adding another cluster stops meaningfully reducing total error. The elbow method is more rigorous and is the standard approach in production data science work.

Is clustering supervised or unsupervised learning?

Clustering is unsupervised learning. It works on data with no labels attached and finds structure purely from the patterns already present in the variables, with no answer key guiding the process.

Which clustering algorithm is best for fraud detection?

Density-based methods like DBSCAN are generally preferred for fraud detection, since fraud typically shows up as a small number of points sitting far outside the normal, dense regions of the data. DBSCAN labels those points as noise automatically, without needing to know how many fraud cases exist ahead of time.

Can clustering algorithms handle outliers?

It depends on the algorithm. K-means is sensitive to outliers because a single extreme point can pull a centroid off course. K-medoids and DBSCAN both handle outliers far better: k-medoids anchors clusters to real data points instead of averages, and DBSCAN explicitly separates outliers into a noise category rather than forcing them into a cluster.
