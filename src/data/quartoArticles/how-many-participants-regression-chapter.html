The Question Every Committee Asks and Almost No Proposal Answers Well

At some point in nearly every quantitative dissertation proposal defense, a committee member asks a version of the same question: “How did you decide on this sample size?” The honest answer, more often than a candidate would like to admit, is some version of “I used a rule of thumb I found online.” That answer rarely survives follow-up questions, because most of the popular rules of thumb turn out to be wrong, or at least far less precise than they sound.

The most rigorous answer to this question in the published literature comes from a 1991 paper by Samuel B. Green in Multivariate Behavioral Research, titled “How Many Subjects Does It Take to Do a Regression Analysis?” It remains one of the most cited papers on sample size for regression analysis in dissertation methodology sections, decades after publication — and what it actually found is more nuanced, and more useful, than the single number most people repeat from it.

The Rules of Thumb You’ve Probably Heard (And Why They’re Incomplete)

Before Green’s paper, researchers commonly relied on fixed rules that ignored context entirely. One rule, attributed to Marks, simply specified a minimum of 200 subjects for any regression analysis, regardless of how many predictor variables were involved or what the researcher was actually trying to detect. Another popular rule, from Tabachnick and Fidell, suggested a minimum of 5 subjects per predictor variable, with 20 per predictor preferred. A third, attributed to Harris, suggested the sample should exceed 50 plus the number of predictor variables.

Green’s contribution was to actually test these rules using statistical power analysis — the formal method for calculating how likely a study is to detect a real effect, given its sample size and the size of the effect being looked for — rather than treating them as folk wisdom. What he found was that most of these fixed rules produce inadequate statistical power to detect meaningful effects under realistic conditions. In plain terms: a lot of dissertations following these popular rules were quietly underpowered, meaning they had a real risk of missing a genuine relationship in their data simply because the sample was too small to detect it reliably — not because the relationship wasn’t there.

What Green Actually Recommended

Green proposed two related guidelines, depending on what the researcher is trying to test:

  • To test the overall model fit (the R², or how much of the outcome your full set of predictors explains together): a minimum sample size of roughly 50 plus 8 times the number of predictors (50 + 8k). For a model with four predictors, that’s 50 + 32 = 82 participants.
  • To test individual predictors within the model (whether a specific variable’s coefficient is meaningfully different from zero): a minimum of roughly 104 plus the number of predictors (104 + k). For four predictors, that’s 108 participants.

Green’s recommendation, further applied in later methodological work, is to calculate both numbers and use whichever is larger — because a dissertation typically needs to report on both the overall model and the individual predictors within it, and being underpowered on either one undermines the chapter.

This is a meaningfully different answer than “200 subjects, always” or “5 per predictor.” It scales with how many predictors you’re actually including, and it distinguishes between two different, equally important questions a regression chapter needs to answer.

Why This Matters Beyond Just Passing a Defense

An underpowered study doesn’t just risk missing a real effect — it risks something more subtle and more damaging to a dissertation’s credibility. Later methodological work building on Green’s approach has emphasized that researchers who use a rule of thumb rather than a formal power analysis trade the simplicity of a fixed number for accuracy and specificity, and that this trade-off has real consequences: a null result in an underpowered study is genuinely ambiguous. It might mean there’s no real relationship, or it might mean the study simply wasn’t equipped to find one that exists. A committee that understands this distinction will ask which explanation applies to your findings, and “I didn’t do a power calculation” is not an answer that holds up.

This is also directly relevant to factor analysis and related techniques used to combine survey items, not just regression. Related research by Guadagnoli and Velicer, examining how sample size affects the stability of factor analysis results, found that the number of participants needed depends heavily on how strongly your items are related to each other — meaning there’s no single safe number that applies across every kind of study, and citing a generic “rule of thumb” without justifying it against your specific design is a gap a careful committee member will notice.

What to Actually Do About This

  • Before you collect a single data point, calculate both of Green’s numbers for your specific number of predictors — 50 + 8k and 104 + k — and use the larger one as your floor, not your target.
  • Report the calculation in your methodology chapter, not just the final number. Showing the reasoning is what actually satisfies a committee member’s question — a bare number without justification invites exactly the follow-up question you’re trying to avoid.
  • If your sample size ends up smaller than what a formal calculation recommends, say so explicitly and discuss it as a limitation, rather than hoping nobody asks. Committees respond far better to an acknowledged limitation than to a gap they have to discover themselves.
  • If you’re running a factor analysis or PCA on survey data with many items, don’t reuse your regression sample-size justification — the Guadagnoli and Velicer research shows these techniques have their own sample size logic tied to how strongly your items correlate with each other.
