The Company That Tried to Replace the Agent

In April 2018, Zillow announced it would start buying houses directly from homeowners. No agent walkthroughs, no open houses, no waiting for a buyer to show up. A homeowner would enter their address, Zillow’s pricing algorithm would generate an instant cash offer, and if the seller accepted, Zillow would close in days, make light repairs, and relist the home itself. The program was called Zillow Offers, and it was built on the same Zestimate technology the company had spent a decade turning into the most recognized home valuation tool in the country. Zillow had more housing data than almost any company on earth. If any business was going to prove an algorithm could do an agent’s job, it was this one.

By 2021, Zillow Offers was buying thousands of homes a month across dozens of markets. Then, in the space of about eight weeks that fall, the company announced it was pausing new home purchases, then announced it was shutting the entire division down, then reported a loss of roughly $304 million in a single quarter tied mostly to homes it had bought for more than it could now sell them for. It laid off about a quarter of its workforce, roughly 2,000 people, and was left holding thousands of homes it needed to unload at a loss just to get them off the books. CEO Rich Barton told investors the algorithm’s price predictions had proven far less accurate than the company believed, particularly once the market moved faster than the model could keep up with.

Zillow did not fail because it lacked data. It had more housing data than any real estate company in history. It failed for three specific, unglamorous reasons, and every one of them shows up in a much smaller form in an individual agent’s business every single week. Zillow was too slow to react to a market that was changing in real time. It ended up sitting on an enormous asset, thousands of physical homes, that was losing value the longer it sat untouched. And it poured its resources into scaling a system built for speed and convenience instead of the harder, more accurate judgment the job actually required. Three failures. One of the largest real estate companies in the country. And, it turns out, the same three places where an individual agent quietly loses deals without ever noticing.

When the Market Moves Faster Than You Do

Zillow’s pricing algorithm was built to update home value estimates using recent sales data. For most of its life, that worked well enough, because home prices in most markets move slowly and predictably. In 2021, they didn’t. Prices in many of Zillow’s target markets accelerated faster than the historical patterns the algorithm had been trained on, and by the time the model adjusted, Zillow was still making offers based on numbers that were already out of date. The company was buying homes at prices set by data that no longer reflected the market it was buying into. Being a step behind a fast moving market, even by a small margin, turned into hundreds of millions of dollars in losses once multiplied across thousands of homes.

The version of this that shows up in an individual agent’s business is smaller, but the mechanism is identical, a fast moving moment where being a step slow costs real money. In 2011, researchers James Oldroyd, Kristina McElheran, and David Elkington published The Short Life of Online Sales Leads in the Harvard Business Review. They didn’t survey companies about how fast they say they respond to a new lead. They submitted real, test web leads to 2,241 U.S. companies and timed what actually happened next.

The results were rougher than most businesses would guess. The average first response time, among companies that responded at all, was 42 hours. Just 37 percent responded within an hour. Another 16 percent took between one and 24 hours. Nearly a quarter, 23 percent, never responded at all. Those leads didn’t go cold because they were bad. They went cold because, from the lead’s side of the exchange, nothing happened, in exactly the same way a homeowner watching prices climb week over week stops trusting an offer built on last month’s numbers.

The finding that should change how any lead driven business operates: companies that made contact within an hour of a lead arriving were about seven times more likely to have a real, qualifying conversation than companies that waited just one additional hour. Companies that waited a full 24 hours were sixty times less likely to have that conversation than the one hour group. An earlier, more granular study makes the same point at a smaller scale. In 2007, researcher James Oldroyd, then at MIT’s Sloan School of Management, partnered with InsideSales.com on the Lead Response Management study, tracking minute by minute response times across six companies, more than 15,000 leads, and over 100,000 phone calls. Contacting a lead within five minutes made an agent 100 times more likely to actually reach the person, and 21 times more likely to qualify them, than waiting 30 minutes. That study measured contact and qualification, not closed sales, and it covers only six companies in one industry, so the 2,241 company Harvard number is the more representative one to plan a business around. Both point the same direction: minutes, not hours, decide whether a lead becomes a real conversation at all.

Real estate leads bought from portals like Zillow and Realtor.com already convert at a thin 0.4% to 1.2% on average. Top performing teams push that to 3% to 9%, largely through faster, more consistent follow up, not better leads. When the starting conversion rate is already under 1 in 100, a factor that multiplies the odds of even having a real conversation by seven times isn’t a nice to have. It’s close to the entire difference between an agent closing one deal out of a hundred leads and one closing five. Zillow had an entire engineering team and a decade of pricing data and still lost hundreds of millions of dollars to a market that moved a few weeks faster than its model could adjust. An agent doesn’t get weeks. An agent gets, on average, about one hour before the odds of a real conversation start collapsing.

The Homes Sitting on the Books

By the time Zillow shut Offers down, it was holding roughly 7,000 homes it had already bought and needed to sell, worth a combined figure the company itself later estimated in the billions. Every one of those homes was an asset that had already cost Zillow real money and was now losing value the longer it sat unsold, through carrying costs, market softening, and the simple fact that a house nobody is actively marketing is a house that isn’t generating any return at all. Zillow ended up selling large batches of that inventory to institutional investors at a loss just to clear it off the books, a forced fire sale of an asset the company itself had chosen to acquire.

Most agents are sitting on their own smaller version of that same problem, and most never notice it the way Zillow eventually had to. Agents tend to treat lead generation as a buying problem: more leads, more ad spend, more portal subscriptions. A 2019 case study by real estate coach Tom Ferry, working with the data platform Revaluate, took a different angle. They pulled roughly 500,000 contacts across eight agent databases and simply checked, when these people eventually sold their home, who did they list with?

Ninety three percent of the time, the answer was a different agent. Across the sample, that pattern was tied to an estimated $2.57 billion in listing volume that agents had already earned the relationship for and lost anyway, in a single 12 month period. The researchers could only confirm about half of those transactions against public records, since the other half lacked a matched address, which means the true number was likely closer to double.

This is a vendor case study, not a peer reviewed academic paper, and it should be read with that caveat. But the direction is corroborated by NAR’s 2025 Profile of Home Buyers and Sellers: 91 percent of buyers say they’d use their agent again or recommend them, yet separate industry tracking cited at real estate coaching events puts the share of past clients who actually follow through and refer new business at closer to 15 percent. Willingness to refer and actually referring are not the same behavior, and the gap between those two numbers is exactly where a database goes quiet without anyone noticing. Nothing dramatic happens. No client complains. They just don’t call back when they’re ready to sell, and they don’t think to mention the agent’s name to a friend, because eighteen months have passed since anyone reached out.

Zillow needed a $304 million quarterly loss to force it to clear its idle inventory. An agent doesn’t need anything nearly that dramatic to notice theirs. The fix costs nothing to set up: a quarterly check in, a market update, a home value alert. It’s not a new lead source. It’s making use of relationships that already exist, the same underused asset problem we cover from a different angle in why your dental practice’s numbers are lying to you.

Building for Scale Instead of Judgment

Zillow Offers was designed around a specific bet: that speed and convenience mattered more than the judgment an experienced agent brings to pricing an individual, idiosyncratic property. The algorithm could process a valuation in seconds and generate an offer without a single human looking at the house. What it couldn’t do was the thing an experienced local agent does instinctively: notice that a renovation was done cheaply, that a floor plan doesn’t suit the neighborhood’s buyers, that a block has quietly become less desirable than the comps suggest. Zillow leadership knew this was a risk going in. They chose to scale the volume of homes bought anyway, because volume was the business model, and pulling back on volume to spend more time on individual judgment would have undermined the entire premise of the product.

Agents make a smaller version of that same choice every week, usually without recognizing it as a choice at all. Not all leads are close to equally valuable, and the gap is bigger than most agents assume when they’re comparing sources on a spreadsheet by lead count alone. REDX’s analysis of 2.7 million seller leads, tracked from May 2024 through January 2026 across all 50 states, found that expired listings, homes that were on the market and didn’t sell, convert at a 44 percent listing rate and a 20.7 percent sold rate, the highest of any lead source they measured, including referrals. For sale by owner sellers convert at a lower but still strong 27.8 percent listing rate. Both categories dramatically outperform an agent’s sphere of influence, which converts at roughly 3 to 5 percent annually according to the same research, and both outperform portal leads by a wide margin.

The reason is simple and doesn’t require a study to explain it: an expired listing has already decided to sell and already tried once. The seller isn’t being convinced to sell their home. They’re being convinced to hire a different agent, which is a much shorter conversation. A brand new portal lead has often only just started thinking about it.

The practical problem is that expired listings and FSBO sellers take more effort to find and more nerve to call than clicking boost on a Zillow subscription, so many agents skip the highest converting category entirely and lean on the lowest converting one because it arrives automatically in an inbox. It’s the same trade Zillow made, choosing the easier, more scalable path over the one that actually required judgment and effort, at exactly the moment judgment and effort would have paid off the most. Tracking cost per closed deal, not cost per lead, makes the real trade off visible: Deal Machine OS’s 2026 lead generation data puts the cost per closing from expired listing prospecting at $300 to $500, versus a blended industry cost per lead across all sources of roughly $448, before that lead has even converted into anything.

What Zillow’s Failure Actually Proves

Zillow Offers did not fail because a smarter competitor beat it. It failed because a company with more housing data than almost anyone alive still couldn’t out execute three basic operational failures: reacting too slowly to a moving market, letting an asset sit idle while it lost value, and scaling the easy part of the job instead of the part that actually required skill. No single one of those decisions sank the division. All three, compounding over about three years, did.

There’s an argument buried in that story that should matter to every individual agent reading it, not despite the fact that it’s about Zillow, but because of it. The largest real estate technology company in the country tried, with real money and real engineering talent, to prove that an algorithm could replace the judgment, relationships, and responsiveness an agent provides. It couldn’t. What actually separates agents who close deals from agents who quietly lose them isn’t access to better data than everyone else has. It’s speed, follow through, and knowing where to spend effort, exactly the three things Zillow’s algorithm couldn’t do at scale and an individual agent still can, one relationship at a time.

  • Build a same hour contact process for every new lead, no exceptions. The Harvard Business Review research suggests this is close to the single highest leverage change available, roughly a seven times difference in the odds of a real conversation between a one hour and a two hour response. Zillow had a full engineering team dedicated to reacting to market changes and still lost hundreds of millions being a few weeks slow. An individual agent doesn’t get weeks.
  • Pull the CRM’s dormant contacts and treat that list as a channel, not an archive. The Tom Ferry and Revaluate case study suggests this is frequently a business’s highest return activity, and the contacts already exist at zero acquisition cost. A quarterly check in is enough to start. Don’t wait for a forced write down to notice what’s already sitting there.
  • Stop judging lead sources by raw lead count. Expired listings and FSBO sellers convert at multiples of what portal leads and sphere contacts do. If none of this quarter’s prospecting time went toward either, that’s the first thing to change, not the marketing budget.
  • Track cost per closed deal, by source, not cost per lead. A source that looks expensive per lead can be the cheapest per closing, and a source that looks free can be the most expensive once the conversion rate is factored in.

If any of this sounds like a dashboard problem rather than a discipline problem, that’s usually because it is: most brokerages already have this data sitting in their CRM and just aren’t looking at it by source. Zillow didn’t need better data to survive Offers. It needed to act on the data it already had, on a timeline that mattered.
