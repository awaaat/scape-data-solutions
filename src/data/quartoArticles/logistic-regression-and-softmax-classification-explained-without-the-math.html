The Score That Replaced a Gut Feeling

In 1956, an engineer named Bill Fair and a mathematician named Earl Isaac started a company on a premise the lending industry found close to offensive at the time: that a statistical formula could predict whether a borrower would repay a loan more reliably than an experienced loan officer’s judgment. For decades, lending decisions had been made by people looking at an application and making a call, informed by experience, sometimes informed by things that had nothing to do with actual creditworthiness. Fair and Isaac’s company, which became known as FICO, built a model that took a set of measurable facts about a borrower, payment history, amounts owed, length of credit history, and combined them into a single number that predicted the probability of default.

The specific statistical method that underlies a modern FICO-style score, and thousands of other yes or no prediction problems across finance, medicine, and marketing, is called logistic regression. It’s worth understanding what it actually does, because the idea is simpler than the name suggests, and it explains something that trips up a lot of people encountering it for the first time: why banks don’t just use ordinary regression, the kind that predicts a number, to predict something like loan default.

Why an Ordinary Regression Line Doesn’t Work for Yes or No

Picture a simple version of the problem: predicting whether a loan defaults, yes or no, based on a borrower’s income. An ordinary regression line, the kind that predicts a continuous number, would draw a straight line through the data and let that line keep going forever in both directions. That’s a problem for a yes or no outcome, because a straight line will eventually predict something nonsensical, a probability of default above 100 percent for a very low income borrower, or below 0 percent for a very high income one. Probabilities can’t go below zero or above one, and a straight line doesn’t know that.

Logistic regression solves this with a specific kind of curve instead of a straight line, an S shaped curve that starts near zero, rises through the middle, and flattens out near one, no matter how far the input values stretch in either direction. That curve is the whole trick. It lets the model take any input, an income level, a credit history length, a debt to income ratio, and always output something that behaves like a genuine probability, a number between zero and one that never breaks the basic rules a probability has to follow. Where an ordinary regression answers “how much,” logistic regression answers “how likely,” and that distinction is why it became the default tool for exactly the kind of yes or no decision a bank has to make on every application.

The model doesn’t just spit out a yes or no answer either. It reports a probability, a 73 percent estimated chance of repayment, for example, and it’s the lender who decides where to draw the line, whether 73 percent clears the bar for approval or whether the threshold sits somewhere else entirely depending on the lender’s risk appetite. That’s a meaningful feature, not a limitation. It means the same underlying model can support very different lending strategies simply by moving where the approval line sits, without needing to rebuild the model itself.

Softmax: The Same Idea for More Than Two Outcomes

Logistic regression, in its basic form, answers a two option question: default or repay, approve or decline, fraud or not fraud. Plenty of real financial classification problems have more than two possible outcomes. A lender assigning a loan into one of five risk tiers, or a bank routing a transaction into one of several fraud investigation categories, needs a version of the same idea extended across more than two options at once.

That extension is called softmax, and it works on the same core logic as logistic regression, just generalized. Instead of producing one probability for a single yes or no outcome, it produces a full set of probabilities across every possible category at once, one for each risk tier or each classification option, and those probabilities are built so they always add up to exactly 100 percent across the full set. A transaction might come out as an 80 percent chance of being ordinary activity, a 15 percent chance of being a false alarm worth a second look, and a 5 percent chance of being genuine fraud, all three numbers produced by the same underlying calculation, all three required to add up to the whole. The category with the highest probability becomes the model’s prediction, but, exactly as with a two option logistic regression, the full set of probabilities is often more useful to a real decision maker than the single predicted label, because it shows how confident or uncertain the model actually is, rather than collapsing that nuance into one final answer.

Why This Still Matters Decades After Fair and Isaac

FICO’s original insight in 1956, that a consistent statistical score beats an inconsistent gut call, has held up remarkably well, and logistic regression remains one of the most widely used methods in finance for a specific reason beyond its accuracy: it’s interpretable. A bank can look at a logistic regression model and say, in plain terms, that a borrower’s debt to income ratio contributed this much to the predicted probability of default, and a specific length of credit history contributed that much, in a way that’s far harder to do with more complex modeling techniques that trade interpretability for a small gain in raw predictive accuracy. In a regulated industry where a lender has to be able to explain why an application was declined, that transparency isn’t a nice to have, it’s frequently a legal requirement, and it’s a large part of why a method invented to formalize the work of a 1956 loan officer is still doing exactly that job today.

  • Use logistic regression when the outcome is yes or no, and you need to explain why. Its S shaped curve keeps every prediction a genuine probability, and its coefficients can be explained in plain language to a regulator, a client, or a committee.
  • Use softmax when there are more than two possible categories. It extends the same core idea across every option at once, always producing a full set of probabilities that add up to the whole.
  • Treat the output as a probability, not just a label. The specific approval threshold is a business decision layered on top of the model, not something the model decides on its own, which is exactly what makes the same model flexible enough to support different risk strategies.
